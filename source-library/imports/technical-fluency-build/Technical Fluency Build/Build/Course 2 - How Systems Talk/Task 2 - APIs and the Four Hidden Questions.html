APIs, and the four hidden questions

The most common scoping claim you'll hear, the four questions it conceals, and why the answer decides whether the work is a week or a quarter.

Course How Systems Talk Read time ~8 min

An API is the contract for asking another system for something. That's the definition. The reason this task exists is that the sentence "we'll just call their API" gets said in scoping conversations constantly, gets written into estimates on the strength of having been said, and turns out to be wrong often enough to be worth four questions of your time.

What an API actually specifies

An API is the contract for asking another system for something. The word gets used as though it means "a way in," but a real API defines six things, and every one of them can be the reason your plan doesn't work:

  • Endpoints — what you can ask for.
  • Request format — how you have to ask.
  • Response format — what comes back, and in what shape.
  • Authentication — how the other system knows it's you.
  • Rate limits — how often you're allowed to ask.
  • Versioning — what happens to you when they change it.

"We'll just call their API"

This is the single most common scoping claim you will hear, and it is not a plan. It's a sentence that sounds like a plan, and it hides four questions that decide whether the work is a week or a quarter.

The hidden questionWhat a bad answer costs you
Does it expose the data we need?The whole feature. Plenty of APIs return most of what you want and not the one field the requirement depends on
At what rate limit?A design change. "10,000 calls a day" and "10 calls a second" are different architectures if you have a million users
With what authentication?Weeks. Enterprise auth is where integration timelines slip — Task 6 covers why
Who owns the credentials?Your start date. Frequently a team that doesn't report to your sponsor and has its own queue

Ask all four before that assumption reaches an estimate. Not after, when the number is already in a document with someone's name on it.

  • "Fine — we'll integrate with their API in sprint three." "Before I put that in the plan: does it expose what we need, what's the rate limit, what's the auth, and whose credentials are we using?"

Four questions, about ninety seconds. They are the highest-value ninety seconds in most scoping conversations, and nobody will think worse of you for asking — the engineers in the room have been waiting for someone to.

Someone else's roadmap, inside your timeline

A third-party API is a vendor dependency with a delivery date attached to it. Rate limits change. Pricing changes. Endpoints get deprecated. The provider has an outage on the morning of your demo. Every one of those sits outside your control and inside your commitment.

Which is why it belongs in the risk register by name — "dependency on [provider] API" — rather than being folded into a general integration line. Named risks get owners and mitigations; unnamed ones get discovered.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

Know that an API is a contract rather than a door, and that "we'll just call their API" is a claim rather than a plan. You don't have to challenge it. You do have to notice that it hasn't been checked.

The move: write the four questions on a card and keep it in your notebook.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

These four questions are yours to ask, and asking them in the scoping conversation is most of the value you add that day. Each one maps to a different failure: missing data kills the feature, rate limits change the design, auth eats the schedule, and credentials decide when you can start.

The move: ask all four before the estimate is written down, not after.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

Third-party API dependency is vendor risk with your delivery date attached. Rate limits, pricing, deprecations, and outages are outside your control and inside your timeline — and an unnamed dependency has no owner. Name the provider in the risk register.

The move: put the provider's name in the risk register, not "integration risk."

Key takeaways

  1. An API is a contract, not a door. Endpoints, request and response format, authentication, rate limits, and versioning — any one of them can sink the plan.
  2. "We'll just call their API" is a claim, not a plan. It sounds like a decision and it hides four unanswered questions.
  3. The four: data, rate limit, auth, credentials. Missing data kills the feature; rate limits change the design; auth eats the schedule; credentials decide your start date.
  4. Ask before the number is committed. Ninety seconds in the scoping conversation, versus a renegotiation later.
  5. Name the provider in the risk register. An unnamed dependency has no owner, and the provider's roadmap is inside your timeline.
