SQL, NoSQL, and the data warehouse

Where a client's data lives, the guarantee they traded away to get scale, and the most expensive sentence in analytics scoping.

Course How Systems Talk Read time ~8 min

Two questions come up on almost every engagement: where does the data live, and why is the dashboard out of date? The answers are the same three concepts, and one of them is a business decision that's routinely made by engineers and never surfaced to the people accountable for it.

The actual difference

SQL databases enforce a schema and give you transactional guarantees, usually summarised as ACID: a transfer either fully happens or fully doesn't. NoSQL relaxes those guarantees in exchange for scale and flexibility, typically offering eventual consistency — the data will agree with itself shortly.

The decision usually turns on one question: does this data have real relationships and correctness requirements?

Says SQLCan say NoSQL
Money, orders, patients, bookingsEvent streams, product catalogues, session data
Anything where two records disagreeing is a problem someone has to clean upAnything where a few seconds of disagreement is invisible

Most real systems legitimately use both, and "which database are you on?" often has more than one right answer. That isn't a mess; it's normal.

"Eventually consistent" is a business decision

This is the sentence to notice. Eventual consistency is a business decision disguised as a technical one. It means that for some window of time, two parts of the system can give different answers to the same question.

For a product catalogue that's fine. For an account balance, a medication record, or a seat on a flight, it's a different conversation entirely — and in finance or healthcare it can be a compliance problem rather than a user-experience one.

Your job isn't to make the call. It's to make sure the client's PM knows which one was picked and what it means for their data. That is frequently news to them, because it was decided by an engineer solving a scaling problem two years ago.

The data warehouse, and why the dashboard says "as of last night"

A data warehouse is column-oriented storage optimised for analytical queries over huge datasets, loaded from the production systems on a schedule. Production databases are built for handling one order at a time; warehouses are built for asking questions about ten million of them.

That scheduled load is why the dashboard is out of date. It's typically a nightly job — Course 3 covers the scheduling mechanism — and it's a design choice you can change, at a cost, if the business genuinely needs fresher data.

Expensive sentence"We want real-time analytics."

Establish what refresh interval the business decision actually requires before anyone prices streaming infrastructure. The honest answer is often "hourly would be fine," and the gap between hourly and real-time is most of the cost.

Regulated client?

If you're engaged with a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services client, an eventual-consistency model on records that carry regulatory weight — balances, medication histories, transaction logs — likely needs legal/compliance review and data-handling constraints before it can move forward the way a vendor's documentation describes. Assume "the data will agree with itself shortly" is a compliance question before it's a technical one.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

SQL for things that must be exactly right; NoSQL for things that must be fast and huge. A warehouse is the separate copy the reporting runs against, which is why the numbers are from last night rather than from now.

The move: when a dashboard looks wrong, check when it last loaded before assuming the data is wrong.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

Two conversations are yours. Making sure the consistency model is understood by the person accountable for the data, and pinning down what refresh interval the business actually needs before "real-time" gets into a requirement.

The move: ask what decision depends on the freshness, then work back to the interval.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

Eventual consistency in finance or healthcare can be a compliance exposure rather than a UX quirk, and it was usually chosen by an engineer solving a different problem. "Real-time analytics" is one of the most expensive sentences in a scoping conversation, and the cost sits between hourly and instant.

The move: price the refresh interval the business can defend, not the one it asked for.

Key takeaways

  1. SQL guarantees correctness; NoSQL trades it for scale. Money, orders, patients, and bookings say SQL. Catalogues and event streams can say NoSQL.
  2. Most systems legitimately use both. "Which database?" often has more than one right answer, and that's normal.
  3. "Eventually consistent" is a business decision in technical clothing. In finance or healthcare it can be a compliance problem, not a UX quirk.
  4. The warehouse is a separate, scheduled copy. That nightly load is why the dashboard says "as of last night."
  5. "We want real-time analytics" is expensive. Find the refresh interval the decision actually needs before anyone prices streaming.
