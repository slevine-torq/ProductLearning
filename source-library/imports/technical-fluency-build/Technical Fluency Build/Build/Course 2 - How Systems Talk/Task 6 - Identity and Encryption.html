Identity and encryption

Two controls clients treat as one, the reason your first sprint disappears, and the team whose queue is your real schedule.

Course How Systems Talk Read time ~8 min

Everything in this course moves data between systems, which raises two questions any client in a regulated industry will ask: is it protected, and who's allowed to see it. These three topics answer that — and two of them carry onboarding costs that show up in your schedule long before they show up in the architecture.

Two encryption checkboxes, not one

Clients routinely conflate these, and knowing the difference makes you useful in a security review rather than a spectator in one.

What it protectsHow
In transitData moving across a networkTLS on the wire — the S in HTTPS
At restData sitting in storageEncrypted disks, databases, and backups

They are separate controls and both are usually required. "We use HTTPS" answers only the first one. A system can encrypt every request perfectly and write it all to an unencrypted database, and that's a finding.

In every regulated Torq industry these are effectively mandatory — HIPAA, PCI DSS, and SOX-adjacent controls all assume both. Absence is a finding with a regulatory consequence attached, not a backlog item to be prioritised against features.

VPN — check this before you diagnose anything else

A VPN is an encrypted tunnel that makes a remote machine appear to sit inside a private network. It is very often the reason an integration works in the client's office and fails from your laptop.

Two practical consequences. First: when something works for them and not for you, check the VPN before you start diagnosing the application. Second, and more expensive:

Plan for itVPN access for consultants at a large enterprise routinely takes one to three weeks.

Build it into the engagement plan or lose the first sprint to it. This is one of the most reliably underestimated items in consulting onboarding, and it's entirely predictable — which means there's no excuse for it appearing as a surprise in week two.

OAuth, SSO, and where integrations actually slip

OAuth 2.0 issues scoped tokens so an application can act on a user's behalf without ever seeing their password. SSO centralises identity through a single provider, so one login works across many systems. Together they're how enterprise software handles "who are you and what are you allowed to do."

And they are, reliably, where enterprise integration projects slip. Provider quirks, group mapping, and user provisioning all take longer than the happy path in the documentation suggests — every vendor's SSO is standard right up until you implement it.

If a deliverable requires SSO integration, treat the client's identity team as a critical-path dependency from day one. They are usually a small, central, heavily-booked team who don't report to your sponsor, and their queue is the real schedule.

Regulated client?

If you're engaged with a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services client, encryption in transit and at rest are effectively mandatory rather than optional — HIPAA, PCI DSS, and SOX-adjacent controls all assume both — and any gap needs legal/compliance review and remediation on a timeline that isn't yours. Assume an encryption gap is a regulatory finding before it's a backlog item.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

Encryption in transit and at rest are two different things and clients mix them up. VPN is why it works for them and not for you. SSO is the thing that takes longer than anyone says.

The move: check the VPN before diagnosing anything that works for the client and not for you.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

You can be useful in a security review by separating the two encryption controls instead of accepting "we use HTTPS" as a complete answer. On identity, your contribution is scheduling — getting the identity team engaged before the sprint that needs them, not during it.

The move: book the identity team in week one for any deliverable with SSO in it.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

Encryption gaps are findings with regulatory consequences in every regulated Torq industry. Identity is where enterprise integrations most reliably slip, which makes the client's identity team a critical-path dependency to be named in the plan. And consultant VPN provisioning is a one-to-three-week onboarding cost that belongs in the mobilisation plan.

The move: put identity-team availability and VPN provisioning in the plan as dated dependencies.

Key takeaways

  1. In transit and at rest are separate controls. "We use HTTPS" answers one of them. Both are usually required.
  2. Missing encryption is a regulatory finding. HIPAA, PCI DSS, and SOX-adjacent controls assume both — it isn't a backlog item.
  3. Check the VPN first. It's the usual reason something works in the client's office and not on your laptop.
  4. Consultant VPN access takes one to three weeks. Plan for it or lose the first sprint to it.
  5. SSO always takes longer than the happy path suggests. Treat the client's identity team as a critical-path dependency from day one.
