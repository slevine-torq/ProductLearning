What tier am I?

Three dials — scope, autonomy, impact — that place you honestly, and one thing the tier model is not.

Course Before You Start Read time ~8 min

Every task in this program presents three tier reads, so it's worth ten minutes to know which one is yours. Tiers here don't split on years served or on a checklist of skills — they split on what the client sees you produce and defend.

What the client room asks of each tier

TierRolesWhat the client room asks of them
Tier 1Associate / Sr. AssociateProduces the artifact. Someone else defends it. Needs to not be the bottleneck in week one.
Tier 2Consultant / Sr. ConsultantOwns a workstream end to end. Writes the spec, defends the roadmap, runs the readout, survives the client's pushback in the room.
Tier 3Principal / AD / DirectorShapes the engagement. Scopes it, sells the next phase, sponsors the deliverable, carries the risk and the money conversation.

The three dials

That table is a description. These are the dials underneath it — the same conclusion Cracking the PM Career (Bavaro & McDowell) reaches independently: level is set by scope, autonomy, and impact, not by a checklist of demonstrable skills.

  • Scope — how big the territory is, and how much of it arrives undefined.
  • Autonomy — how much support you need to get through that territory.
  • Impact — what came out the other end, and what it changed.
ScopeAutonomyImpact
Tier 1A defined slice, with the hard questions already settled by someone elseRuns on supplied rails — a template to follow and a reviewer before it shipsThe deliverable is correct and lands on schedule
Tier 2A whole workstream, unsettled parts includedGiven an objective rather than instructions, and holds the output up under client pushbackSomething the client was going to do got done differently
Tier 3The engagement itself — its boundaries and what it costsDraws the lines other people work within; the person the client negotiates withThe relationship grew, or how the client operates changed

The dials often disagree, and that's the useful part. Full autonomy on a scope someone handed you is a real, common position — and it tells you which dial is lagging.

Tier 3 is not a deeper technical level

This is the misreading that would break the rest of the program, so it's worth being blunt about. Tier 3 does not mean more engineering depth than Tier 2. A Principal reading a client's architecture is working from the same facts a Senior Consultant has — they are attaching a different question to them.

Given the same factTier 2 asksTier 3 asks
"We're on a Java monolith, no CI/CD."What does that do to release cadence, and what would it take to change?What does that do to the estimate, the hiring plan, and what phase two can credibly promise?
"Everything goes through one vendor's API."What breaks if it's slow or down, and is there a fallback?What's the lock-in, the renewal leverage, and who carries that risk in the SOW?

A different axis, not a higher one. Neither Tier 3 column requires knowing more about the JVM than the Tier 2 column does. It means connecting the same L3 facts to money, time, and risk. If you ever find yourself thinking "I'll get the commercial read once I'm technical enough," that's the misreading talking — the commercial read is a separate thing to learn, and you can start on it now.

How the dials move

The mechanism that follows: you reach the next tier by demonstrating autonomy and impact at your current scope, which earns the trust to be handed a larger one. Not by collecting skills off a rubric. Scope is granted; autonomy and impact are demonstrated. That's why waiting for a bigger scope to prove you deserve a bigger scope goes nowhere.

Scoping this into a phased SOW is usually an AD/Director call rather than yours. Knowing how the decision gets made is what lets you feed it good input now — and make it yourself later.

All three reads, always visible

Find yours, then read the other two anyway — that's how you know what the conversation above you sounds like.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

Place yourself honestly, then read the Tier 2 column as a description of the job you're moving toward — not as a verdict on you. Most consultants underplace themselves on impact because they're counting deliverables rather than decisions changed.

The move: ask your lead which dial they'd move first if it were their call.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

Check the dials against your current staffing, not your title. Being handed a pre-scoped slice on one engagement and owning ambiguity on the next is normal, and the tier that matters is the one this engagement is asking for this week.

The move: name the ambiguity you're carrying out loud, so the client knows it's owned.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

The dials are a staffing instrument. Reading them across a team tells you who can be left alone with an ambiguous workstream and who needs the scope cut cleaner — which is a delivery-risk question before it's a development one.

The move: staff against the autonomy dial rather than the rate card.

Key takeaways

  1. Tiers split on what the client sees you produce and defend. Not years served, not a skills checklist.
  2. Scope, autonomy, impact are the three dials. They frequently disagree, and the disagreement is the useful signal.
  3. Tier 3 is not a deeper technical level than Tier 2. Same facts, attached to money, time, and risk — a different axis.
  4. You don't need to be "technical enough" to start the commercial read. It's a separate thing to learn, available now.
  5. Scope is granted; autonomy and impact are demonstrated. Which is why you move by proving them at the scope you already have.
