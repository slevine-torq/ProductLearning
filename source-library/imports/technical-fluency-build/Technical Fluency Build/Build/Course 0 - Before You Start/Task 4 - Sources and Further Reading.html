Sources & further reading

Where this program's content actually comes from, what it deliberately isn't, and the two limitations worth knowing before you quote any of it to a client.

Course Before You Start Read time ~7 min, or use as a reference

One attribution page for the whole program rather than a citation block on every task. Two reasons. An idea's origin is part of the information, and some of what follows has no source-book equivalent — which you should know before you lean on it.

What this program is not

  • Not a coding course. Nothing here teaches you to write anything. The ceiling is L3 — understanding what a technical choice implies for the product.
  • Not an architecture opinion. Recognition earns you the right to ask a good question, not to critique a client's stack. That's the one rule from Task 1, and it's the reason this page exists in the shape it does.
  • Not current forever. The AI and agentic material (Course 5) ages in months. Courses 1–4 age in years. Re-verify Course 5 before quoting anything to a client.
  • Not a substitute for asking. The best outcome here is that you ask a better question, sooner, of the person who actually knows.

Further reading, by what you'd want it for

  • The five-level fluency ladder (Task 1) Deepak Singh, Tech Simplified for PMs and Entrepreneurs (2022) — the cleanest available framing of self-placement from "no clue" to "you write the code," and the source of this program's category ordering.
  • Scope, autonomy, impact; the five currencies; the rubric critique (Tasks 2–3) Jackie Bavaro & Gayle Laakmann McDowell, Cracking the PM Career (2021) — for level being set by scope, autonomy and impact rather than a skills checklist; for splitting capability into five separately-earned currencies; and for the argument that conventional ladders are either too vague to act on or circular. Also the source for Senior being a legitimate place to stop, which is where Task 3's "Tier 2 is a destination" framing comes from.
  • Influence without authority Treated across the same book as the defining skill of the tier that owns a workstream end to end — and the reason Torq's own learning path assigns its influence-focused Product Leadership module at Tier 2 rather than Tier 3.
  • Every technology entry in Courses 1–5 Primary vendor and project documentation, plus public engineering write-ups from the teams that run these systems at scale. Where a vendor's documentation and a public post-incident write-up disagree on what something costs in practice, this program follows the write-up.
  • The reference guide itself Technical Fluency Reference Guide — Torq's own, and the lookup reference that survives alongside this program. Courses 1–6 teach from it; keep it open during client standups rather than re-reading tasks.

Two honest limitations

  1. The source book is India- and consumer-internet-centricTorq's client base is Fortune 500 enterprise, much of it regulated. The enterprise and legacy-system framing throughout this program is Added content, not source coverage — treat it as Torq's read, tested against your engagement, rather than as an external authority.
  2. Nothing here replaces the engagement's own AI and data policyBefore any tooling from Course 5 touches client material, confirm the engagement's approved tools and data-handling rules. The TorqHub "AI at Torq Intro" course is the prerequisite for every tier of the Learning Path, and it carries the actual policy. This program does not.
Regulated client?

If you're engaged with a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services client, anything in this program touching data movement, third-party APIs, cloud hosting, or AI tooling likely needs legal/compliance review, a longer approval cycle, and data-handling constraints before it can move forward the way a vendor's documentation describes. Assume "we'll just call their API" and "we'll put that in the model's context" are both compliance questions before they're technical ones.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

Read the master table in the reference guide once, end to end, then keep it open during client standups. That single habit does most of the work of "don't be surprised," and it's cheaper than re-reading tasks.

The move: bookmark the guide, not this page.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

The Origin column matters to you specifically. When you're about to say something out loud in a client session, knowing whether it's a canonical definition or this program's own framing tells you how hard to hold it under pushback.

The move: hold Industry-standard rows firmly; hold Added rows as an opening question.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

Anything from this program that reaches a proposal, an estimate, or a client-facing deck needs its source checked first — particularly Course 5, which ages in months. An Added row quoted as industry consensus in an SOW is a credibility risk with a commercial cost attached.

The move: re-verify before it's written down for a client, not after.

Why this page exists

Two reasons. First, good scholarship: naming where an idea actually comes from is more useful than presenting it as unattributed common knowledge. Second, it's the honest record of what this program can and can't vouch for — the Added rows have no external authority behind them, and you should know that before you quote one in a client room.
