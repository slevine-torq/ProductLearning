The frontend/backend split

Why a tiny change sometimes costs a fortnight, and how to predict which kind you're asking for before you commit to a date.

Course How Software Is Built Read time ~8 min

Four tasks of vocabulary come together in one question. The split between frontend and backend isn't only a technical boundary — it's usually different languages, different skills, different teams, and different release cadences. Which makes it the answer to the question you will be asked more than any other: why does this tiny change cost so much?

What the split actually is

Frontend is what runs in the user's browser or on their phone. Backend is the logic and the data behind it. They talk over an API. That much you had by the end of Task 1.

The part that costs money is organisational. Those two halves are frequently built by different people, hired from different pools, working to different release schedules, sometimes in different time zones and occasionally at different companies. The estimate follows the team topology, not the code.

"Just add one field"

Here is the same-sounding request in two versions. Both take about four seconds to say.

Layer"Change this button to blue""Add the customer's tier to this screen"
Database—Possibly a migration, reviewed and scheduled
Backend logic—Fetch it, validate it, decide who's allowed to see it
API—A new field in the response, possibly a new version so existing clients don't break
FrontendOne value, one fileDisplay, empty states, tests
ReleaseShips on the frontend team's next deployCoordinated across two teams, in dependency order

Nobody was being obstructive in the right-hand column. The request genuinely crosses four layers and two teams, and the person who said "just add one field" heard a four-second sentence and priced it that way.

Learning to predict which one you're asking for

This is the highest-value skill in the whole reference guide, and it's a skill rather than a fact — you get it by guessing and checking, repeatedly, until your guesses get good.

The rough heuristic: if the information is already on the screen, changing how it looks is usually cheap. If the information isn't there yet, something has to go and get it, and that's the expensive shape. Not always right. Right often enough to be worth saying out loud, framed as a question.

  • "This should be a quick one." "Is this frontend-only, or does it need something new from the backend?"

The second version costs nothing if you're wrong and saves an argument if you're right. It also does something subtler: it tells the engineering team you know the difference exists, which is most of what they want from a product person.

Release cadence, and the cost nobody estimates

When the two halves release on different schedules, every cross-cutting feature carries coordination cost: the backend change has to land first, the frontend waits for it, and if either slips the other waits again. That cost is real, recurring, and routinely missing from estimates — because it isn't work anyone does, it's time everyone spends waiting.

You can find this out in one question in week one, and it will explain more about a client's delivery speed than almost anything else you could ask.

Regulated client?

If you're engaged with a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services client, a coordinated multi-team release likely needs a change-approval board, a fixed release window, and evidence of testing before it can move forward the way this task describes. Assume that a change touching the database and the API is a scheduling question before it's an engineering one, and get the window booked early rather than discovering it exists in the final week.

Tier 1 — a level deeper

Know that "small change" and "small change" can be two completely different requests, and that the difference is whether anything new has to come from the backend. Stop predicting; start asking. The question is free.

The move: "frontend-only, or does the backend need to change?" — every time, until you can guess it.

Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss

You're expected to predict this before it reaches an estimate, and to say so when a stakeholder prices a four-second sentence at four seconds. Practise on real backlog items and check yourself with an engineer; being wrong in a corridor is free.

The move: walk the layers out loud before committing to a date.

Tier 3 — strategic & risk read

Team topology sets the estimate. Two teams on different cadences means every cross-cutting feature carries coordination cost that estimates omit, and across a programme that gap is where overruns come from. It's also an argument for a specific organisational change, which is a larger conversation than a delivery one.

The move: put coordination cost in the estimate as a line, not as optimism.

Key takeaways

  1. The split is organisational before it's technical. Different languages, skills, teams, and release schedules — and the estimate follows the teams.
  2. "Just add one field" can cross four layers and two teams. A four-second sentence is not a four-second change.
  3. Predicting which kind you're asking for is the highest-value skill here. You get it by guessing, checking with an engineer, and being wrong cheaply.
  4. If the data is already on the screen, changing it is usually cheap. If something has to go and fetch it, that's the expensive shape.
  5. Different release cadences add cost nobody estimates. It isn't work anyone does; it's time everyone spends waiting.
