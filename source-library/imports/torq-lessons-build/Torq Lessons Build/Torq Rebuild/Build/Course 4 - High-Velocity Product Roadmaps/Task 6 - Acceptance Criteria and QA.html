Acceptance criteria and QA

A PRD tells a builder what to build. Acceptance criteria tell whoever tests it — who almost never wrote the PRD — exactly what "done" means, in terms they can check without guessing.

Course High-Velocity Product Roadmaps Read time ~8 min Artifact Acceptance Criteria Checklist
A Torq addition

This task isn't adapted from the source certification — it's built specifically for Torq's own work. A consultant embedded on a client account almost always hands work off to a QA function that wasn't in the room when the PRD was written, which makes this gap real in a way it usually isn't for a product manager who owns delivery start to finish.

On your own team, "looks right to me" might be enough to ship. Handing a feature to a client's QA analyst — someone who never saw your PRD get written, doesn't know your persona, and is testing against a checklist, not a vibe — is a completely different situation.

Why a PRD alone isn't enough to test against

A PRD's smart behaviors and functional requirements describe intent. Acceptance criteria translate that intent into something a tester can execute step by step and mark pass or fail — no interpretation required, no need to have been in the room when the requirement was written.

  • "The system should handle errors gracefully." "When a required field is left empty and the user clicks Submit, an inline error message appears next to that field within one second, and the form does not submit."

    • The second version needs no follow-up conversation to test. The first one guarantees one.

    A format that travels well

    Given / When / Then Given [the starting state], when [the user does this], then [this specific, observable thing happens].

    This format works for almost any functional requirement or smart behavior in your PRD — it forces a starting state, a trigger, and an outcome specific enough to actually check. Write one per requirement, not one broad paragraph per feature.

    What UAT actually confirms

    User acceptance testing isn't "does this work" — that's what QA already checked. UAT confirms the feature does what the business needed, from the perspective of someone who'll actually use or approve it. On a client engagement, that's often a formal, named step before a milestone is considered complete — and skipping straight from "QA passed" to "done" is a common way handoffs go wrong.

    Regulated client?

    On a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services engagement, UAT is frequently a compliance requirement, not just a courtesy step — sometimes with its own sign-off document. Confirm this early; it affects your timeline, not just your test plan.

    Key takeaways

    1. A PRD describes intent; acceptance criteria describe a test. Handing off only the first one guarantees a round of clarifying questions.
    2. Given/When/Then works for almost any requirement. It forces a starting state, a trigger, and a specific, checkable outcome.
    3. UAT is a business check, not a repeat of QA. It confirms the feature does what was actually needed, not just that it functions.
    4. On a regulated engagement, UAT may carry its own sign-off requirement. Confirm that early — it changes your timeline, not just your test plan.
