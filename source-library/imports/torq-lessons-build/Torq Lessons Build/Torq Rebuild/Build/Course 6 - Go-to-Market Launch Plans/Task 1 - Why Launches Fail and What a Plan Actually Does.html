Why launches fail, and what a plan actually does

A great product with no launch plan is forgotten in a week. This final course is where everything you've built in Courses 1–5 becomes something a client actually sees ship.

Course Go-to-Market Launch Plans Read time ~9 min Artifact Launch Checklist Draft

Building the right thing and shipping it well are two different disciplines. History is full of strong products that landed with barely a ripple, and rougher ones that landed hard because the plan around them was airtight.

Four launches, four lessons

Landed

Apple iPhone (2007)

A single, tightly controlled reveal built months of anticipation, paired with a product that delivered on the story it told. The launch moment and the product experience matched.

Landed

OpenAI's ChatGPT (2022)

Released with almost no traditional marketing spend — the product itself, made freely accessible, did the work. Sometimes the launch plan is removing every barrier between the product and someone trying it.

Stumbled

Coca-Cola's "New Coke" (1985)

A reformulation backed by real taste-test data still triggered a public backlash strong enough to force a reversal within months — a reminder that a launch plan needs to account for attachment and identity, not just preference data.

Stumbled

Google Glass (2013)

Strong underlying technology launched into a social and privacy context nobody had planned for. The product worked; the go-to-market didn't account for how it would actually land in public.

Two patterns hold across all four: the launch moment and the actual product need to tell the same story, and a launch plan has to account for how something lands in the real world — not just whether it works.

What a launch plan actually covers

A launch plan isn't a marketing afterthought bolted onto a finished product. It's seven concrete decisions, made deliberately rather than by default:

  1. Goal & audienceWhat does this launch need to accomplish, and for whom specifically — not "everyone."
  2. Launch tierHow big a moment is this — a quiet rollout or a full campaign? (More in Task 2.)
  3. Channel planWhere the audience will actually hear about it — owned, earned, or paid. (Task 2.)
  4. Enablement & assetsWhat Sales, Support, and Customer Success need before day one, not after the first ticket comes in. (Task 3.)
  5. Budget & resourcesWho owns spend and headcount for this launch, and what's actually been approved. (Task 3.)
  6. TimelineThe sequence from beta through the launch moment to post-launch monitoring. (Task 5.)
  7. Success metricsHow you'll know it worked, tied back to the goal you set first. (Task 5.)
One level up

Budget and resourcing decisions for a launch are frequently made at the AD or Director level, not by the consultant running day-to-day delivery — often as part of how the engagement itself got scoped into the SOW. You don't need to own that call to do this course well, but understanding that it exists, and who makes it, is part of operating credibly on a launch.

Key takeaways

  1. A launch plan is seven concrete decisions, not a marketing afterthought. Each one made deliberately, not by default.
  2. The launch moment and the product need to tell the same story. New Coke had good data and still lost the room.
  3. Working technology isn't enough on its own. Google Glass shows a launch plan has to account for how something lands socially, not just whether it functions.
