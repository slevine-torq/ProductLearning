Reading the discovery filter

Most engagements don't start with a blank page — they start with a folder of research someone already ran. Your first job is deciding what in it actually deserves the team's time.

Course Discovering Product Opportunities Read time ~8 min Artifact Discovery Filter Audit

An AI tool can summarize a thousand interviews before lunch. That was never the hard part. The hard part is deciding which of the surfaced problems is actually worth solving — and that judgment call doesn't get automated away.

What AI actually compresses

The mechanics of discovery have gotten faster. What hasn't changed is who's accountable for the call at the end of it.

The synthesis got cheap. The judgment about what the synthesis means didn't — and that's still entirely on you.

The discovery filter

Before any research finding gets treated as a real opportunity, run it through three questions. Skip this step and you'll end up chasing whatever surfaced loudest, not whatever matters most.

  • Does it point toward where the account is actually headed? A finding can be true and still be a distraction if it pulls toward a direction nobody's actually committed to this cycle. Is this on the path we're already funded to walk?
  • Does it clear the bar for real resources? Plenty of findings are true and minor. This is your license to say "noted, not now" to something real but small. Would fixing this actually justify pulling engineering off something else?
  • Do you know what winning looks like? If nobody can say what a good outcome is before you start, you won't be able to tell afterward whether it worked. What number, specifically, moves if this gets fixed?

A finding that clears all three is worth a slot on the roadmap. One that clears one or two is worth writing down and revisiting — not worth a sprint yet.

Weighing the evidence you're handed

Inherited research rarely comes labeled with how much to trust it. Two questions fix that fast.

What they said, versus what they did

Interviews and surveys tell you what people believe about their own behavior, which is a real signal but an unreliable one — people are bad at predicting what they'll actually do. Session recordings, support-ticket volume, and usage data tell you what happened, which is harder to argue with. When the two disagree, behavior usually wins.

What stage of certainty it was gathered for

Stage What it's good for
ExploratoryUnderstanding a problem before anything's been designed — open interviews, shadowing, diary studies
EvaluativeTesting something that already exists — usability sessions, session replays, A/B results
Ongoing measurementTracking whether a metric is moving over time — surveys at scale, product analytics, lightweight recurring interviews

A one-off exploratory interview from eighteen months ago is weak evidence for a decision today. A metric that's been tracked continuously since then is strong evidence — know which kind you've inherited before you cite it in a client conversation.

Key takeaways

  1. AI compressed the synthesis, not the judgment. Deciding what a finding means and whether it's worth acting on is still entirely your call.
  2. Every finding clears three tests before it's a priority. Direction, resources, and a defined win — missing any one means "not yet," not "no."
  3. Behavior beats belief when the two disagree. What someone did is harder to argue with than what they said they'd do.
  4. Old evidence isn't equal evidence. Know what stage it was gathered for before you cite it as proof of anything current.
