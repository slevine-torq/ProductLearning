## Task 1 — Reading the Discovery Filter

### Block: Discovery Key Terms — Type: Card flipping

1. Term: Discovery filter | Definition: The three questions any research finding has to clear before it's worth resourcing — direction, resources, and a defined win.
2. Term: Say vs. do | Definition: What someone tells you about their own behavior (less reliable) versus what they're observed actually doing (more reliable). When the two disagree, behavior usually wins.
3. Term: Exploratory research | Definition: Research aimed at understanding a problem before anything's been designed — open interviews, shadowing, diary studies.
4. Term: Evaluative research | Definition: Research that tests something that already exists — usability sessions, session replays, A/B results.
5. Term: Ongoing measurement | Definition: Research that tracks whether a metric is moving over time — surveys at scale, product analytics, recurring lightweight interviews.

### Block: Filter Check — Type: Quiz

1. A finding is true but only affects a handful of low-value accounts. What does the discovery filter tell you?
   A. Ignore it entirely
   B. It doesn't clear the "resources" test — worth noting, not worth a sprint yet ← correct
   C. Escalate it immediately
   D. The filter doesn't apply to small findings

2. Which is stronger evidence when a stakeholder's account and the usage data disagree?
   A. The stakeholder's account, because they know the client best
   B. The usage data — what people did is harder to argue with than what they said ← correct
   C. Neither — average them
   D. Whichever came first

3. What's the risk of citing a research finding from eighteen months ago as current proof?
   A. None — findings don't expire
   B. It may have been gathered at a different certainty stage than what you're using it for now ← correct
   C. It's automatically wrong
   D. Old research is always more reliable

## Task 2 — Personas and the Shape of the Signal

### Block: Jobs, Pains, Gains — Type: Card matching

1. Prompt: Job | Match: What someone is actually trying to accomplish — the underlying goal behind a feature request.
2. Prompt: Pain | Match: What's specifically getting in their way right now.
3. Prompt: Gain | Match: What changes for them, concretely, if the pain gets solved.
4. Prompt: Duolingo's Streaks | Match: Built from a job — users weren't quitting from difficulty, they quit when daily progress plateaued.
5. Prompt: Dropbox for Business | Match: Built from a pain — teams were already hacking shared folders together as makeshift infrastructure.
6. Prompt: Canva's Magic Resize | Match: Built from a gain — automating manual resizing let users publish ten times faster.

### Block: Signal or Noise — Type: Card classification

**Buckets:** Produces signal, Produces noise

1. "Walk me through the last time that happened." → Produces signal
2. "Would you use a feature that did X?" → Produces noise
3. "How are you handling this today?" → Produces signal
4. "What would your dream version of this look like?" → Produces noise
5. "What have you already tried to work around this?" → Produces signal
6. "How much would you pay for this?" → Produces noise

## Task 4 — The Status Quo Is Your Real Competitor

### Block: Status Quo Key Terms — Type: Card flipping

1. Term: Status-quo bias | Definition: A well-documented tendency to overweight the risk of a new option relative to the risk of doing nothing, even when the current state is clearly worse.
2. Term: Breaking point | Definition: The moment the cumulative cost of a workaround finally outweighs the discomfort of switching away from it.
3. Term: Strategic gap | Definition: What has to be true for a workaround to become genuinely unnecessary — not just for a feature to sit next to it.
4. Term: "No decision" | Definition: The most common way an initiative loses — not to a competing vendor, but to the account choosing to keep doing what it's already doing.

### Block: Real Competitor or Not — Type: Quiz

1. A client's real alternative to buying your solution is usually:
   A. A cheaper competing vendor
   B. Staying with their current workaround ← correct
   C. Building it themselves, always
   D. Waiting for a better version of your product

2. Your initiative ships a new feature, but the client's spreadsheet workaround is still in daily use. What does that tell you?
   A. The launch was a success
   B. The strategic gap wasn't actually closed ← correct
   C. Users need more training
   D. The feature needs more marketing

## Task 5 — Reading and Building a Journey Map

### Block: Map the Stage — Type: Card classification

**Buckets:** Set-up, Mapping, Diagnosis

1. "Set the goal" → Set-up
2. "Choose one persona" → Set-up
3. "Define the scope" → Set-up
4. "Outline the steps" → Mapping
5. "Add the touchpoints" → Mapping
6. "Spot the key moments" → Diagnosis

### Block: Journey Map Key Terms — Type: Card flipping

1. Term: Journey map | Definition: A record of what a user does, thinks, and feels at each step of a task — valuable almost entirely for where the emotional line drops.
2. Term: Touchpoint | Definition: Any place a user interacts with the product, the team, or an outside tool — friction often hides in the handoffs between them.
3. Term: Key moment | Definition: A point on the map where the emotional line dips or spikes — the map's actual payoff.
4. Term: Current state | Definition: How the journey works today, workarounds included. Never blend this with the future state on the same map.
5. Term: Future state | Definition: What the journey looks like once a workaround has been made genuinely unnecessary, not just supplemented.
