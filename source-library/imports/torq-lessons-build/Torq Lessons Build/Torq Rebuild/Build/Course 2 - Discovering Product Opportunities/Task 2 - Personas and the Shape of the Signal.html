Personas and the shape of the signal

A persona built from demographics tells you who someone is. A persona built from behavior tells you what they're stuck on — and only one of those is useful for prioritization.

Course Discovering Product Opportunities Read time ~8 min Artifact Behavioral Persona Card

Two users can look identical on paper — same age, same title, same company size — and want completely different things from the same product. Age and title never explain that gap. What they're trying to accomplish, and what's currently getting in the way, does.

Behavior is the anchor, not the bio

A persona sheet full of demographic detail feels thorough and tells you almost nothing about what to build. The useful version is built from two things instead: the goal someone's actually working toward, and the specific workaround they've built to get there despite the product's limits.

Illustrative pattern

Picture a learning platform where two very similar-looking users behave completely differently: one is motivated by finishing a structured path and earning a visible badge for it, the other just wants a fast answer to one specific question and will abandon anything that makes them work through a sequence first. Same product, same price tier — but a completion tracker delights the first and annoys the second.

The product implication: ship one default experience tuned to whichever behavior is more common and valuable, and treat the other as an explicit trade-off you're aware you're making — not something you accidentally optimized against.

You won't always get to build for both. Deciding which behavior gets the default experience is itself a prioritization call, driven by which one hurts more or matters more to the business — not by which one is louder in the room.

Sorting the evidence: jobs, pains, gains

Raw feedback arrives as noise. Sorting it into three buckets is what turns it into something you can act on: the job someone's trying to get done, the pain stopping them, and the gain they'd get if it were solved.

  • Job — what they're actually trying to accomplish Duolingo noticed users weren't quitting because lessons were too hard — they quit once daily progress plateaued. The job wasn't "learn the language," it was "keep a streak going," and that reframing led directly to Duolingo's Streaks feature.
  • Pain — what's actively in the way Dropbox saw teams hacking shared folders together as an improvised collaboration tool. The pain was real infrastructure work happening on top of a product that wasn't built for it — which became the direct case for Dropbox for Business.
  • Gain — what they'd get if it were solved Canva users were manually resizing the same design for every platform, over and over. The gain from automating it was concrete and measurable: publish ten times faster. That became Magic Resize.

Notice none of these came from someone asking for the feature by name. Streaks, Dropbox for Business, and Magic Resize all came from watching the job, the pain, or the gain — not from a feature request.

Questions that produce real signal

The questions you ask determine whether you get evidence or opinion. Anchoring in something that actually happened beats asking someone to predict their own future behavior.

Produces signal Produces noise
"Walk me through the last time that happened.""Would you use a feature that did X?"
"What have you already tried to get around this?""What would your dream version of this look like?"
"How are you handling this today?""How much would you pay for this?"

The left column asks about a real, remembered event. The right column asks someone to imagine a hypothetical and then accurately predict their own future behavior — something people are reliably bad at, no matter how confident the answer sounds.

Key takeaways

  1. Behavior beats demographics as a persona anchor. What someone's trying to do and what's blocking them predicts product decisions; age and title don't.
  2. Jobs, pains, and gains turn noise into something actionable. Duolingo, Dropbox, and Canva all found their answer in one of the three — not in a feature request.
  3. Ask about the past, not the hypothetical future. A remembered event is evidence; a prediction about imaginary behavior usually isn't.
  4. You won't build for every behavior at once. Deciding which one gets the default experience is a real trade-off, not an oversight.
