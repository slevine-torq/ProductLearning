Choosing metrics that matter

Any dashboard can show you a number going up. Your job is knowing which numbers actually prove something changed for the better — and which ones just feel good to report.

Course Analytics & Metrics for Product Decisions Read time ~8 min Artifact Metric Quality Check

A metric going up isn't automatically good news. Total clicks, total signups, total dollars moved — all of these can climb while the thing they're supposed to represent gets worse. Learning to tell the difference is most of what this course teaches.

Vanity metrics versus real ones

A vanity metric — a term that entered product vocabulary through Eric Ries's early startup writing — measures an action. A real metric proves an outcome. Total dollars moved through a new payment feature counts clicks; the percentage of users who still hold a higher balance thirty days later proves the feature actually helped someone save. Only one of those survives being reported to a skeptical stakeholder.

The test: could the number go up while the user is objectively worse off? If yes, it's measuring activity, not value.

Three questions, three traps

Before you commit to tracking anything, run it through three questions — and watch for the three ways experienced people still get fooled.

  • What does this actually prove — usage, or value? A metric can climb because a feature got used, without proving anyone's situation improved. Know which claim you're actually making.
  • Can your work actually move it? Total revenue moves for a hundred reasons that have nothing to do with your release. Pick something narrow enough that a change in it is attributable to what you shipped.
  • What are you willing to let get worse? Every real metric needs a counter-metric watching for collateral damage — a spike in signups that quietly tanks retention isn't a win, it's a trade you didn't notice you made.
TrapWhat it looks like
Chasing activityReporting a click-through rate as if it were proof of value
Numbers without researchA metric moves and nobody can say why — the "why" only ever lives in qualitative evidence, not the dashboard
Trusting the averageAn overall number holds steady while it's actually masking one segment doing great and another quietly churning

Leading indicators beat lagging results

A lagging result tells you what already happened — by the time it moves, the outcome is locked in. A leading indicator is something you can influence today that predicts the lagging result later. For a new onboarding flow, seven-day retention is lagging — a week gone by the time it drops. The percentage of users who complete the first real action in session one is leading, and it's the one you can actually push on this sprint.

Win the leading indicator and you've bought yourself a real shot at the lagging one. Chase the lagging one directly and you're just watching a number you can't move.

Key takeaways

  1. A rising number isn't automatically good news. Test whether it could climb while the user is objectively worse off.
  2. Every metric needs a counter-metric. A win that quietly breaks something else isn't a win — it's an unnoticed trade.
  3. Only track what your work can actually move. A metric too broad to attribute to your release is a metric you can't act on.
  4. Leading indicators are where the leverage is. A lagging result only tells you what already happened.
