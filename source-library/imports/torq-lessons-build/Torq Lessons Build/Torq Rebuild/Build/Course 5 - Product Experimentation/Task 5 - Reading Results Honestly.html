Reading results honestly

A dashboard showing "significant" doesn't mean you're done. The number is the easy part — the discipline is in the five questions you ask before you trust it.

Course Product Experimentation Read time ~8 min Artifact Results Reality-Check

A number on a dashboard isn't a decision. It's a claim — and like any claim, it deserves a few pointed questions before you let it change what your team builds.

Five questions before you trust a result

  1. Did it actually reach the sample size you planned for?

    A test cut short — because it "looked done" or a deadline pushed you to call it early — hasn't collected enough data to separate a real effect from noise. Stopping early because the number is moving the way you want is the single easiest way to fool yourself.

  2. Is the primary metric the one that moved, or did you go looking?

    Check the metric you locked in Task 3 first. If it's flat but a secondary metric jumped, that's a new hypothesis for a future test — not evidence for this one. Scanning a dashboard until something turns green is how false positives get shipped.

  3. Did any guardrail move in the wrong direction?

    A primary-metric win that quietly costs you on a guardrail isn't a clean win. Check every guardrail you named before launch, not just the one you were hoping to see move.

  4. Would this result hold up outside this specific slice?

    A win driven entirely by one device type, one region, or one traffic source is a narrower finding than "ship it everywhere." Check whether the effect is broad or concentrated before generalizing it.

  5. Could something else explain it?

    A marketing push, a seasonal pattern, or an unrelated bug fix that landed the same week can all produce a metric shift that has nothing to do with your test. Rule out the obvious confounders before crediting the variant.

The instinct to trust the first good number

This is the same instinct Task 1 named as the whole reason experimentation exists in the first place — a confident read of a screenshot. Passing your own result through these five questions is holding yourself to the same standard you'd hold anyone else's gut call to.

Key takeaways

  1. A dashboard result is a claim, not a decision. Five specific questions separate a trustworthy read from a convenient one.
  2. Stopping early is the easiest way to fool yourself. Reaching the planned sample size isn't optional.
  3. A guardrail slip cancels a headline win. Check every metric you locked before launch, not just the one that moved.
