Making the call

A trustworthy result still isn't a decision. Once you've passed the reality-check in Task 5, you land in exactly one of four places — and each one calls for a different next move.

Course Product Experimentation Read time ~9 min Artifact Call Recommendation

Reading a result and deciding what to do about it are two different skills. This is the second one.

Four outcomes, not two

It's tempting to collapse every result into "it worked" or "it didn't." That loses the two most useful outcomes in between.

Ship

Primary metric moved as predicted, guardrails held, effect held up across segments. Roll it out.

Iterate

Directionally right but not enough to ship as-is — a guardrail dipped slightly, or the effect was real but small. Refine the variant and test again.

Investigate

The result doesn't match what you expected — flat where you predicted movement, or a segment split that makes no sense. Don't ship or kill yet; find out why first.

Kill

No effect, or a guardrail broke. The idea was worth testing and is now worth dropping — that's the system working, not a failure.

"Investigate" is the one teams skip most often, because a clean ship/kill call feels more decisive. But forcing an ambiguous result into one of the other three is exactly the kind of shortcut Task 5's reality-check exists to catch.

Two calls, worked through

Composite scenarios built from common, publicly-discussed testing patterns — not any single company's real reported numbers.

Ship

A mid-size media-streaming app tests autoplay-next

A consumer streaming product tested automatically queuing a related item after the current one ended, against a control that required a manual tap to continue. The hypothesis: reducing that one tap would lift session length without hurting how people rated their experience.

The primary metric — average session length — moved up meaningfully in the variant, held across device types and the two largest markets, and reached the planned sample size on schedule. The guardrail — an in-app satisfaction rating collected after each session — stayed flat, not down. Call: ship, with a follow-up watch on unsubscribe rate over the following quarter, since a session-length win driven by passive autoplay is worth confirming it isn't quietly annoying people in a way the immediate rating doesn't catch.

Investigate

A large online marketplace tests a one-tap reorder button

An e-commerce team tested adding a one-tap "buy again" button to a past-orders page, against the existing multi-step reorder flow. The hypothesis: removing friction would lift reorder completion rate.

Reorder completions rose overall — but the lift was concentrated almost entirely in one product category and nearly flat everywhere else, which didn't match the team's prediction of a broad effect. The overall guardrail (return rate) held. Call: investigate before shipping broadly. A result that strong in one slice and flat elsewhere is more likely explained by something specific to that category — pricing, inventory, or how that category's page was laid out — than by the button itself. Shipping on the headline number alone would have missed that the win wasn't actually coming from the change being tested.

Key takeaways

  1. Four outcomes, not two. Iterate and Investigate are real calls, not a failure to decide.
  2. Investigate is the outcome teams skip. A forced ship/kill call on an ambiguous result undoes the discipline from Task 5.
  3. An uneven result across segments is a clue, not just a footnote. Where the effect is concentrated often tells you what actually caused it.
