Common mistakes in A/B testing

These aren't edge cases. They're the six ways a well-run experiment produces a confident, wrong answer — and every one of them is avoidable with a habit, not a statistics degree.

Course Product Experimentation Read time ~8 min Artifact Pre-Launch Mistake Check

Large-scale experimentation teams — Microsoft's among the most widely published — have documented these failure patterns for years. None of them require advanced statistics to avoid. They require catching yourself doing something that feels perfectly reasonable in the moment.

Six ways a good test goes wrong

  1. Tracking too many metrics at once The more metrics you watch, the more likely one moves by chance alone. Fix: one primary metric, decided before launch — everything else is a guardrail or a secondary signal, not a second chance to call it a win.
  2. Peeking at results early and stopping when you like what you see Checking a live dashboard daily and calling the test the moment it looks good inflates false positives — this is the same discipline Task 5 named as question one. Fix: commit to the planned sample size and duration before launch, and don't call it early because the trend looks favorable.
  3. Launching without a defined success threshold Deciding what counts as "good enough" after seeing the number is rationalizing, not deciding. Fix: the hypothesis formula from Task 3 exists precisely so the bar is set before the data exists.
  4. Reading the topline and skipping segments A flat or positive topline can hide a segment that moved sharply in the wrong direction. Fix: check segment-level results as standard practice, not only when the topline looks surprising.
  5. Avoiding tests that touch the same area of the product at once, out of fear they'll interfere Interaction effects are real, but the fear of them often just means fewer tests get run — a real cost with no result to show for it. Fix: know how your platform handles overlapping tests, and design around it deliberately instead of avoiding testing that area altogether.
  6. Treating one test as the final word A single result — especially a marginal one — can be noise. Fix: a genuinely important decision deserves a second confirming test before it locks in a permanent product change.
The pattern underneath all six

Every one of these is a version of the same thing: letting what you want the result to say influence how you look at it. That's not a character flaw — it's exactly why the checklists in Task 5 and Task 6 exist as external, mechanical checks rather than a judgment call in the moment.

Key takeaways

  1. All six mistakes share one root cause. Letting what you want the result to say shape how you look at it.
  2. These are documented, well-known failure patterns. Large experimentation teams have written about them publicly for years — you're not the first to hit them.
  3. The fix is nearly always "decide before you look." Metric, threshold, sample size, and duration — all set before launch, not adjusted after.
