Choosing the right experiment method

Four tools, not one. Picking the wrong one wastes traffic on a question it was never built to answer.

Course Product Experimentation Read time ~8 min Artifact Method Selection Worksheet

Four experiment types cover almost everything you'll need. They're not competitors — most real launches combine two or three of them, each doing a different job in the same rollout.

Four methods, four jobs

MethodWhat it's forReal-world practice
A/B testMeasure the effect of one specific changeNetflix has publicly discussed testing thumbnail styles against each other — whichever drives more plays wins
MultivariateFind the best combination of several changes at onceTesting image size, price placement, and a CTA label together is common practice at scale — some combinations outperform their parts, which A/B alone won't catch
Feature flagControl who sees a change, safelyA staged 0%→100% rollout with automatic rollback on error spikes is standard practice at large engineering orgs
Canary releaseCatch instability before it reaches everyoneShipping to a small slice first and watching for problems before ramping further is a common deployment pattern

A flag controls exposure. An A/B test measures whether the change actually works. A canary protects everyone else while you find out. Most serious rollouts use more than one of these at once.

Matching the method to the situation

Three questions decide which method fits: what are you actually trying to learn, how much traffic do you have, and how much risk can this take on?

MethodQuestion it answersTraffic neededRisk to production
A/B testDoes the new version beat the old one?Roughly 1,000+ users per armLow
MultivariateWhich combination works best?Roughly 10,000+ usersMedium
Feature flagCan I safely expose this to a segment?Any sizeVery low
Canary releaseIs this stable at scale?Any sizeVery low
Low traffic or high stakes? Start safe

A flag or a canary costs you almost nothing and works at any scale. Save the A/B test for when you have real traffic and a specific hypothesis, and only reach for multivariate when the sample size actually supports it.

Key takeaways

  1. Four methods, four different jobs. They're rarely a choice between one or the other — most real rollouts combine several.
  2. Match the method to your question, traffic, and risk. Not to what's fastest to set up.
  3. Flags and canaries are nearly free. Reach for an A/B test only once you have real traffic and a sharp hypothesis.
  4. Multivariate needs real sample size. Without it, you'll get noise dressed up as an insight.
