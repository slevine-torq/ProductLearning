Your problem hook & value proposition

One sentence that fuses the business risk and the user's pain — the argument every initiative needs before it gets resourced.

Course Strategic Product Thinking Read time ~8 min Artifact Problem Hook & Value Proposition Worksheet

This is the commitment moment. An initiative doesn't get resourced because it's a good idea — it gets resourced because someone made the case that not doing it costs something specific, starting now. That case is one sentence, not a slide deck.

Pick the initiative you'll carry

Choose one real initiative from your current engagement — ideally one you're actively trying to get funded, prioritised, or taken seriously. You'll build on it through the rest of this program: the discovery work, the metrics, the roadmap, the spec, and the readout all attach to the same thing.

Pick something with genuine ambiguity around it. An initiative everyone already agrees on won't teach you much, and it won't stress-test the frameworks.

The strategic crisis

If nothing changes for the next 12 months, what does the business actually lose? Not "things could be better" — a specific, named cost: market position, revenue, a client relationship, a compliance finding, a renewal at risk.

  • Engagement could be higher. If usage doesn't recover by Q3, we go into the renewal conversation without a case.

Vague crises don't get funded — they compete with fifty other backlog items and lose. Named costs create a deadline someone else also cares about.

The moment of misery

What specific workaround is a user forced into right now because the current state fails them? A spreadsheet nobody trusts, a manual re-entry, an email chain, a competitor's tool creeping into the workflow. This is the human proof that the crisis is real rather than theoretical.

  • The tool is slow and people don't like it. The coordinator re-enters the same data into three systems every morning before the shift starts.
Regulated client?

The moment of misery can be a compliance risk rather than a user-facing frustration — a manual control that's one missed step from an audit finding. Same rule: name the specific failure, not a general sense of exposure.

The problem hook

Fuse the crisis and the misery into a single sentence. If it takes three, you haven't found it yet.

HOOKWe must solve [business risk] by addressing [user pain].

The value proposition

Say who it's for, what changes, and why it can't wait.

FOR[who]
WE WILL[value]
BECAUSE[urgent why now]

No technical detail yet — just the strategic tension and the human frustration. Solutions come later; if the why isn't sharp, no solution will save it.

The cold-read test

Walk away for an hour, then come back and read it cold, the way someone hearing it for the first time in a steering meeting would — with none of the context you'd normally fill in out loud. If it doesn't land on its own, that's the finding.

  • Would this actually get funded on its own, or only as a nice-to-have riding along with something else? Needing a sales pitch for why it matters means the crisis isn't sharp enough yet.
  • Is the moment of misery systemic, or just an edge case? One person's complaint isn't a moment of misery. A pattern across a team is.
  • Read it back with the obstacle covered up — does it still make sense, or could it be pitching almost anything? If nothing's lost when you hide the friction, it isn't specific enough yet.

Optional and worth it: hand the hook to a colleague cold, with no setup, and watch which part they ask about first. That's the weak seam.

Optional · dig deeper

Stress-test an unfamiliar model. Draft a second hook for an initiative in a business model you know less well — if your engagement is enterprise, try a consumer product, or the reverse. Can you still find the moment of misery and the strategic crisis when the domain isn't familiar? That's the skill travelling, rather than your existing context doing the work.

Key takeaways

  1. The hook fuses risk and pain in one sentence. If it takes three sentences, keep cutting.
  2. The moment of misery has to be real and specific. Name the actual workaround, not a general sense that something's suboptimal.
  3. Cold-reading your own hook is the cheapest QA available. A skeptical stakeholder will find the soft spot eventually — better that you find it first.
  4. A value prop that could describe any feature isn't specific enough. Generic value props get generic funding decisions.
