The modern PM mindset

Every request arrives labeled urgent. Your value isn't how fast you process them — it's turning that noise into a clear, defensible direction.

Course Strategic Product Thinking Read time ~8 min Artifact Three Pillars Backlog Audit

A product owner's day is a flood of requests — from sales, from support, from an executive who cornered someone in a hallway. All of them urgent. The question this task answers: what actually keeps a product team from moving forward, and what's your job in fixing it?

The clarity problem

Almost all of that friction has one root cause: nobody has agreed on a single direction the team is optimizing for. Without it, every request looks equally urgent, which means none of them is actually prioritized — and a backlog that keeps growing faster than it shrinks is usually a symptom of that, not a capacity problem.

Throughput isn't what makes you valuable here. What does is clarity — pulling a conversation away from how fast can we ship this and back toward why does this matter to the business.

Try this on your own work

Think of a product team you've been on — client or internal — where work felt scattered and directionless. Was that really a resourcing problem, or was it that nobody could say which single outcome the quarter was actually for? That distinction is the whole subject of this course.

From output to outcome

The most important shift you'll make as a PM: stop counting what shipped and start proving what changed. A feature that lands on time, exactly as specced, is an output. Whether it actually made a user's life better is the outcome — and it's the only one that was ever the point.

This isn't "shipping doesn't matter"

Execution is still the job — nothing changes for a user until code ships. The point is that a shipped feature and a solved problem aren't the same claim, and only one of them is worth reporting as a win.

Desirability, viability, feasibility

Remember that flood of requests? This is the filter you run them through. Every real initiative has to clear three separate tests — not just one — and the highest-impact work is what clears all three at once.

  • Desirability Does a real user actually have this problem, and does it hurt enough that they'd change behavior to fix it? If we build it, does anyone care?
  • Viability Does solving it justify the resources — revenue protected, cost avoided, or a strategic position defended? Does this move something the business is actually accountable for?
  • Feasibility Can it be built, shipped, and kept running with the time, skills, and constraints actually available? Can we build and sustain this, not just demo it once?

The overlap is never clean

  • The desirable, feasible fix that has no business case behind it.
  • The viable, feasible initiative nobody asked for and nobody will use.
  • The desirable, viable idea that would take a rebuild to actually ship.

Two out of three is the common case, not the exception. Naming which lens is missing turns a vague "not now" into a specific, arguable reason — that's what makes the call defensible instead of just felt.

Regulated client?

On a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services engagement, feasibility carries a fourth question the other two don't: what has to be reviewed or certified before this can ship? Fold that into the feasibility lens rather than discovering it late.

Key takeaways

  1. A shared North Star is the antidote to "everything is urgent." When nothing is prioritized, the problem is usually clarity, not capacity.
  2. Your value is clarity, not throughput. Move the conversation from the how of shipping to the why of the business.
  3. Outcomes over outputs — but shipping still matters. Success is a measurable change in user behavior, not a date on a calendar.
  4. Desirability, viability, feasibility make judgment defensible. They don't remove the trade-offs; they give you a reason you can say out loud for how you resolved them.
