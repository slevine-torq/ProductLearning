The product development life cycle

The industry-standard map from a hunch to a finished product — a sequence of checkpoints designed to catch a wrong bet before it's expensive to unwind.

Course Strategic Product Thinking Read time ~10 min Artifact Lifecycle Health Check

Every org calls the stages something slightly different, and none of that matters much. What holds constant underneath the naming: catch a bad idea while it's still cheap to kill, hold a quality bar while it's being built, and treat launch as a checkpoint, not a finish line.

Your safety net

This is the structure that keeps you from becoming an order-taker shipping whatever landed loudest this week. Every stage exists so that, months later, you can point to exactly why a call got made the way it did.

What you actually do at each stage

One real feature traced through the whole cycle below: Netflix's "Play Something" button — a real, publicly discussed example of a product moving from a vague signal to a measured outcome.

Early stages

Discover

Discovery · Research
  • Turn a pile of scattered signals into one specific problem statement
  • Decide which of the candidate problems is actually worth solving first
  • Agree on what "this worked" would look like — before anyone starts building

Goal: earn the right to spend design and engineering time by proving the opportunity first.

Netflix: a PM notices users scrolling for twenty minutes without pressing play on anything — the question that opens: how do we get someone watching faster?

Execution stages

Build

Design · Development · Testing
  • Keep design and engineering pointed at the problem, not just the ticket
  • Trade off scope against the calendar without quietly losing the original goal
  • Check the build against both "does it work" and "does it solve the thing"

Goal: ship something that actually resolves the user's problem, not just something that compiles.

Netflix: the team prototypes where a "Play Something" control would live, then ships the simplest version of the logic first to keep the loop fast.

Final stages

Deliver

Launch · Iteration
  • Coordinate the release with whoever else touches the customer
  • Watch the metric you actually committed to, not a convenient substitute
  • Make the call: keep investing, change direction, or walk away

Goal: get it in front of real users and let their behavior — not your instinct — decide what happens next.

Netflix: "Play Something" ships to a small slice first; the team checks whether time spent deciding actually dropped before rolling further.

Moving through it faster

None of the stages above disappear with AI in the loop. What changes is how much of each one is grunt work versus a real decision — the grunt work compresses, the decision doesn't.

PhaseYouAI assists withOutput
Discover Input qualitative feedback and strategy Analysing hours of research video and text A summary doc with key pain points and quotes
Design Define strategic trade-offs and success criteria Converting insights into PRD drafts; generating prototype code A multi-section PRD and an interactive prototype
Deliver Make launch decisions and plan GTM strategy Drafting GTM copy, blog posts, test scenarios A launch-ready checklist and campaign materials

The loop closes faster each cycle: every human decision is backed by synthesised evidence, and every plan is supported by rapid, high-quality artifacts. What doesn't change is who decides.

Regulated client?

On a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services engagement, Testing and Launch often carry a formal compliance or approval gate that isn't optional. Build that time into your estimate early — don't let it surface the week before launch.

Key takeaways

  1. The lifecycle exists to catch a wrong bet early, not to add process. Each stage is a checkpoint where a bad idea is still cheap to kill.
  2. Agree on "success" before the build starts. Retrofitting a definition of done after the fact almost always favors whatever shipped.
  3. Launch is a checkpoint, not a finish line. Watch the metric you committed to, then actually make the call — double down, pivot, or walk away.
  4. AI compresses the grunt work, not the judgment. It can draft and synthesize at speed; deciding what's actually launch-ready still has to be you.
