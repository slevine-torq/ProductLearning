Sources & further reading

Where the frameworks in this program actually come from — and where to go deeper on any of them.

Read time ~4 min, or use as a reference Artifact None — reference only

This program teaches real, established product-management thinking — not a proprietary framework invented for this course. Every named concept below has a real originator, and this page exists so you can trace it back and read further. It's built incrementally: one entry per course, added as that course ships.

Course 1 · Strategic Product Thinking

  • Outcome vs. output, and the "feature factory" trap John Cutler coined "feature factory" to describe teams measured on shipping rather than impact. Marty Cagan (Inspired, Silicon Valley Product Group) and Teresa Torres (Continuous Discovery Habits) are the standard references on building outcome-driven product teams.
  • Desirability, viability, and feasibility The three-lens framework for evaluating what to build, from design thinking. Popularized by Tim Brown (IDEO), notably in "Design Thinking" (Harvard Business Review, 2008) and Change by Design.
  • The product manager's cross-functional position The idea of the PM as the connective role across a cross-functional team — not an authority figure, but the one person tracking the whole picture — is core to Cagan's writing on the PM role and widely echoed across product-leadership literature.
  • Netflix's product and personalization practices Referenced as a real, publicly documented example of shipping against a clear business outcome. Not a proprietary Torq case — drawn from Netflix's own public statements about its product culture.
  • Value propositions and jobs-to-be-done Alexander Osterwalder et al., Value Proposition Design, for the for/gain/pain structure behind a value proposition. Clayton Christensen's "jobs to be done" theory for framing what a customer is actually hiring a product to do.

Course 2 · Discovering Product Opportunities

  • Attitudinal vs. behavioral research, and research-type taxonomies "What people say" vs. "what people do" is a standard distinction in UX research — see the Nielsen Norman Group's writing on the topic. Erika Hall's Just Enough Research is a standard reference for structuring a research practice around foundational, evaluative, and strategic questions.
  • Jobs, pains, and gains as a discovery lens Alexander Osterwalder et al., Value Proposition Design — the same framework cited in Course 1, applied here to sorting raw discovery signal into insight.
  • Status-quo bias, and "no decision" as your real competitor Samuelson & Zeckhauser, "Status Quo Bias in Decision Making" (1988), for the underlying behavioral-economics finding. Matthew Dixon & Brent Adamson, The Challenger Sale, for the observation that a buyer's inertia is often the real competing option, not a rival product.
  • Journey mapping A standard UX and service-design method for tracing a user's experience across touchpoints — see the Nielsen Norman Group's guidance on journey mapping.

Course 3 · Analytics & Metrics for Product Decisions

  • Vanity metrics vs. strategic metrics Eric Ries, The Lean Startup (2011) — the term and the underlying caution against metrics that move without reflecting real business health.
  • Actionable, accessible, auditable metrics Alistair Croll & Benjamin Yoskovitz, Lean Analytics — the standard for what makes a metric worth tracking, including the counter-metric concept for catching a win that costs you somewhere else.
  • The AARRR funnel ("Pirate Metrics") Coined by Dave McClure (500 Startups, ~2007) — Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue.
  • Leading indicators vs. lagging results Standard business and OKR vocabulary, predating any single course — see Kaplan & Norton's Balanced Scorecard work for one of its earliest formal treatments.
  • Hypothesis-driven product decisions The build-a-testable-hypothesis-before-you-build discipline traces back to Eric Ries's Lean Startup methodology.

Course 4 · High-Velocity Product Roadmaps

  • The effort/value matrix Widely-taught prioritization vocabulary across product management — not attributable to a single originator, but a standard tool in the field.
  • The 70/20/10 innovation split Google's publicly described model for allocating engineering time across core, adjacent, and new-bet work.
  • MVP (minimum viable product) Term coined by Frank Robinson, popularized industry-wide by Eric Ries's The Lean Startup.
  • MoSCoW prioritization Created by Dai Clegg at Oracle (1994), formalized through the DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method) consortium.
  • Build-Measure-Learn Eric Ries's core Lean Startup loop, applied here to moving from a validated hypothesis through a PRD to a working prototype.
  • The "press release" technique for writing requirements Associated with Amazon's "Working Backwards" PR/FAQ process, as described by former Amazon product leaders including Ian McAllister.

Course 5 · Product Experimentation

  • A/B testing, multivariate testing, feature flags, canary releases Four well-established experimentation methods used broadly across the software industry — not any single vendor's proprietary framework. Optimizely's publicly documented A/B testing workflow is a standard reference for the setup sequence.
  • Common statistical mistakes in A/B testing — peeking, multiple comparisons Extensively documented by Ronny Kohavi and the experimentation research team he led at Microsoft — see Kohavi, Tang & Xu, Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020).
  • The hypothesis formula for a test The same Lean-Startup-style hypothesis pattern cited under Course 3 and Course 4, applied here to a single experiment.

Course 6 · Go-to-Market Launch Plans

  • Apple iPhone, OpenAI's ChatGPT, Coca-Cola's "New Coke," Google Glass Four real, well-documented product launches used as the module's core teaching — genuine business history, not a proprietary case study.
  • Owned, earned, and paid media A standard channel-strategy framework, commonly traced to Forrester Research's work on media models.
  • "Pivot" Coined by Eric Ries, The Lean Startup — the same source cited under Course 3 and Course 4, applied here to a post-launch decision: changing direction while keeping what was validated.
Why this page exists

Two reasons. First, good scholarship: naming where an idea actually comes from is more useful than presenting it as unattributed common knowledge. Second, it's the honest record of this program's own rebuild — every framework here was rewritten from its real industry source rather than adapted from any single training vendor's packaging of it.
