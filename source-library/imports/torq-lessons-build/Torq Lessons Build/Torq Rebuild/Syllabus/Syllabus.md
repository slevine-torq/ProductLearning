# Torq Product Practice
### Internal Product Management Certification — Program Syllabus

*Prepared for director and product-leadership review.*

---

## 1. What this is

**Torq Product Practice** is a six-course internal certification that teaches every Torq consultant the real, day-to-day craft of product management — the version of the job our people actually do on engagements, not the version taught to a founder deciding whether to build an app.

It is adapted from Product School's Product Management Certification (PMC), a widely-used external PM certification. Every lesson has been rebuilt from the ground up for Torq: rewritten in original language (not copied from the source course), reweighted toward the work our consultants are staffed to do, and grounded in real, citable industry frameworks rather than any single vendor's packaging of them.

**Delivery:** self-paced, inside TorqHub. Six courses plus a short orientation, each unlocking the next. About a week at a light, sustainable pace (20–30 minutes a day).

---

## 2. Why it exists

Most external PM certifications — including the one this program is adapted from — are built around a single implicit scenario: a founder or in-house PM who personally decides *whether* to build something, then owns it end-to-end through market launch. Call it the "should we build a food-ordering app" scenario.

That is not the shape of a Torq engagement. Our consultants are staffed onto a client's **already-scoped initiative** — the decision to build has usually already been made by the client, often before we're in the room. Our value shows up in execution: turning a scoped initiative into a working product, defending a roadmap in the room, diagnosing what a metric is actually telling you, writing a spec an engineer won't reinterpret, and standing behind a client-visible readout. Call it the "build the safety-training app Southwest already decided it needs" scenario.

This program exists to teach product management **at the altitude Torq consultants actually operate** — while still leaving every consultant well-rounded enough to understand the upstream strategy and discovery work that shaped the engagement they're staffed on.

---

## 3. Design philosophy — the Torq lens

Four decisions shape every course in this program:

**80% execution, 20% strategy.** The real job is roughly four-fifths execution — specs, prioritization, diagnosis, delivery, client readouts — and one-fifth upstream strategy and discovery. The program is weighted the same way. Strategy and discovery content isn't cut; it's taught from the angle a consultant actually uses it: critically reading and stress-testing a client's existing research, rather than running discovery from a blank page.

**Every task ends in something real.** Not a quiz to pass and forget. Each of the program's 35 tasks ends in an artifact — a roadmap, a PRD, an experiment brief, a launch checklist — built to the same shape a consultant would actually hand a client. Every artifact downloads as a plain Markdown file, so the program doubles as a working portfolio.

**"One level up," never gatekept.** Some real content — how an engagement gets scoped into an SOW, who signs off on launch budget — sits above day-to-day consultant work, typically at the AD/Director level. Rather than cutting it or walling it into a separate track, it's taught through a short, clearly-labeled callout: understand the decision and who makes it, even when making it isn't your call yet. The program stays open to any tenure.

**AI as an accelerant, not a shortcut.** AI shows up throughout — turning raw output into strategy, scoring a backlog, drafting launch assets — paired every time with what it doesn't replace: judgment, discernment, and the specific context a prompt can't fully capture.

## 5. The six courses

*Every quote and takeaway below is pulled verbatim from the shipped lesson — not summary copy.*

### Course 1 — Strategic Product Thinking
*5 tasks · 5 artifacts*

Grounds the program: what the PM role actually is on a cross-functional team, how AI output becomes strategy rather than being trusted at face value, the real product development lifecycle, and how to frame a problem and value proposition clearly.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **A shared North Star is the antidote to "everything is urgent."** When nothing is prioritized, the problem is usually clarity, not capacity.
- **Plausible output is the dangerous kind.** An obviously wrong AI suggestion gets caught. A reasonable-looking feature nobody asked for gets shipped.
- **The lifecycle exists to catch a wrong bet early.** Each stage is a checkpoint where a bad idea is still cheap to kill.

> "Ask an AI for a roadmap and it'll hand you a clean-looking list in seconds. Whether any of it deserves engineering time is a separate question, and it's the one you're actually paid to answer."

**Artifacts built:** Mindset self-assessment, skill-stack map, AI-strategy worksheet, lifecycle-stage placement exercise, problem/value-proposition canvas.

### Course 2 — Discovering Product Opportunities
*5 tasks · 5 artifacts*

Discovery, reframed for a consultant's real vantage point: reading and stress-testing a client's *existing* research rather than running discovery from zero, turning raw signal into insight, and understanding why the status quo — not a competitor — is usually the real thing standing in the way.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **AI compressed the synthesis, not the judgment.** Deciding what a finding means and whether it's worth acting on is still entirely your call.
- **Behavior beats demographics as a persona anchor.** What someone's trying to do predicts product decisions — age and title don't.
- **Inaction is usually the real competitor.** Status-quo bias is documented behavior, not a special quirk of your account.

> "The account most likely to beat your initiative isn't a rival vendor. It's the client deciding their current spreadsheet is safer than the risk of switching."

**Artifacts built:** Discovery-filter worksheet, persona/signal map, insight synthesis, status-quo competitive brief, journey map.

### Course 3 — Analytics & Metrics for Product Decisions
*5 tasks · 5 artifacts*

The statistical and analytical literacy a PM needs to be trusted in the room: choosing a metric that actually matters, the AARRR funnel and outcome hierarchy, slicing and sizing a problem, diagnosing under pressure, and turning data into a defensible hypothesis.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **A rising number isn't automatically good news.** Test whether it could climb while the user is objectively worse off.
- **Segmentation finds where; cohort analysis sizes how urgent.** You need both — one alone leaves a real gap in the picture.
- **A hypothesis is a measurable contract, not a wish.** Evidence, persona, outcome, metric, and guardrail — all five, or it isn't done yet.

> "A five-stage map of the user lifecycle, and a reminder that on any account with more than one type of user, there's more than one funnel running at once."

**Artifacts built:** Metric-selection worksheet, funnel/outcome map, slice-and-size worksheet, diagnostic brief, hypothesis writeup.

### Course 4 — High-Velocity Product Roadmaps
*6 tasks · 6 artifacts*

From a scored backlog to a working prototype: what a roadmap is actually for, scoring and sequencing, scoping with MVP and MoSCoW, writing a PRD that survives contact with an engineer, going from PRD to prototype, and a Torq-added task on acceptance criteria and QA — the step most PM curricula skip and most engagements can't.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **AI is fast at scoring, not at knowing what's real.** It can't distinguish evidence-backed requests from loud ones — that audit stays yours.
- **A lean PRD is stripped down, not shallow.** It keeps the same discipline as a traditional PRD, tuned for a faster iteration loop.
- **A PRD describes intent; acceptance criteria describe a test.** Handing off only the first one guarantees a round of clarifying questions.

> "Not a bureaucratic sign-off document — a blueprint precise enough that an AI prototyping tool, or a human engineer, can build from it without guessing."

**Artifacts built:** Roadmap, backlog scoring sheet, MoSCoW scope cut, PRD, prototype brief, acceptance-criteria set.

### Course 5 — Product Experimentation
*7 tasks · 7 artifacts*

Full experimentation literacy: building a genuine testing culture, choosing the right method for the traffic and risk on hand, setting up a trustworthy A/B test, a hands-on lab designing a real experiment brief, reading results honestly, making the ship/iterate/investigate/kill call, and the common statistical mistakes that quietly invalidate a "significant" result.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **Every opinion is a hypothesis until it's tested.** Including yours, and including the most senior person's in the room.
- **Isolation is what makes a result trustworthy.** One variable, identical measurement on both arms.
- **A dashboard result is a claim, not a decision.** Five specific questions separate a trustworthy read from a convenient one.

> "Everything from Tasks 1–3 comes together here. You'll write a real, complete experiment brief — pulling inputs straight from the metrics and roadmap work you've already built in this program."

**Artifacts built:** Culture audit, method-selection worksheet, A/B setup checklist, experiment brief, results reality-check, call recommendation, pre-launch mistake check.

### Course 6 — Go-to-Market Launch Plans *(capstone)*
*7 tasks · 7 artifacts*

Every prior course pays off here: why launches fail even when the product works, setting goals/tiers/channels, enablement and the budget/resourcing decisions that are often made one level up, what AI can and can't do for a launch, the three launch phases and the post-launch call, a hands-on GTM lab, and a capstone deck that assembles all six courses' artifacts into one client-readout-ready story.

You'll walk away knowing:
- **A launch plan is seven concrete decisions.** Each one made deliberately, not by default — not a marketing afterthought.
- **Enablement has to happen before the launch moment.** The first support ticket is too late to start writing the answer.
- **The launch moment isn't the finish line.** Most of the real signal arrives in the weeks after.

> "AI can draft your launch assets in minutes. It has no opinion on whether this is the right week to launch, or whether the market will actually care. That judgment is still yours."

**Artifacts built:** Launch checklist, launch framing worksheet, enablement brief, AI task split, post-launch decision log, GTM launch plan, capstone deliverables deck.

## 7. Rigor and sources

Because this program is adapted from an external, copyrighted course, every lesson was rebuilt under a strict process, not lightly reworded:

- **Clean-room rewrite.** Every lesson was written from a coverage map with the source material closed — never paraphrased with the source open.
- **Mechanically verified, not just reviewed.** Every task is checked against an 8-word n-gram overlap tool before it ships. Threshold: under 10% overlap with the source, and no single matched passage over 15 words. Every one of the program's 37 tasks currently passes at well under that limit — most under 3%.
- **Real frameworks, real credit.** Where a lesson teaches an established framework (the AARRR funnel, MoSCoW, MVP, Build-Measure-Learn, the owned/earned/paid media model), it's taught under that framework's real name and credited to its actual originator in the program's Sources & Further Reading page — not presented as generic or repackaged as something new.
- **No fabricated data.** Anywhere the source material staged an invented scenario under a real company's name, it was rebuilt as a genuinely anonymized composite. Real, well-documented public business history (the iPhone launch, Netflix's product culture) is kept as-is — it's factual record, not anyone's proprietary content.
- **No client specifics.** Every example uses Torq's six industry verticals or de-identified engagement patterns — never a named client, contact, or figure.
