## Slide 7

### AI-UX Implementations

- Traditional AI-UX
- AI-Native UX
- AI features at the product core, built from the ground up, where the model is the engine of the workflow.
- Intent-driven interaction where the interface evolves in real-time to surface only the tools the AI predicts are needed.
- Offers seamless, personalized user experiences that minimize manual navigation and instructional labor.
- EXAMPLE: PerplexityAn AI-first search engine where responses are generated directly by AI, offering context-aware results.
- EXAMPLE: Microsoft BingAI-powered answers added to a traditional search engine to enhance search results with conversational AI.
- AI features retroactively layered top of a legacy, deterministic workflow to add incremental efficiency.
- User-driven interaction where the user must manually trigger AI actions within a static interface.
- Enhances current products by adding valuable AI-driven enhancements without disrupting familiar habits.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> It's important to note that while traditional AI-UX can certainly provide value by improving existing processes, building AI-native products from the ground up often creates a more seamless and intuitive user experience. When we talk about AI-native UX, we’re not just integrating AI into an existing product but building experiences where AI capabilities are at the core of the design. The difference here is subtle but impactful.
> Traditional AI-UX
> Traditional AI-UX is an "additive" approach where you layer AI onto deterministic, instruction-based workflows to add incremental efficiency.
> In this model, your interface remains static and your user is still responsible for the "labor" of the workflow—manually triggering the AI to summarize or filter data.
> Think of this as your traditional toolbox retrofitted with a smart sensor; the tool hasn't changed, but you can now provide extra data on top of the existing process.
> For example, Microsoft Bing retrofitted AI-powered answers into their legacy search engine that still relies on traditional keyword indexing and page-rank layouts.
> AI-Native UX
> When you build AI-native UX, you start from a "Clean Sheet" where the model is the engine of the entire workflow rather than just a feature.
> Your goal here is to shift your user from "Instruction" to "Intent," where the interface dynamically generates the exact Micro-UI needed to fulfill a goal in real-time.
> This approach removes friction by eliminating manual navigation, allowing your system to anticipate needs and surface synthesis instead of just raw data.
> For example, Perplexity is built as an AI-native engine where the interface is a generative synthesis of information, completely replacing the legacy list-of-links interaction model

## Slide 8

### Lowered Interaction Costs

- Why AI-Native UX Matters
- AI-native UX isn't about better buttons; it's about uncapping user potential by automating the manual execution that limits their impact.
- Increased System Resilience
- Contextual Relevance
- Reduces the friction of task completion by moving your user from manual “searching” to “reviewing and deciding”.
- Replaces repetitive instructional labor with automated synthesis, freeing your user to focus on high-level judgment.
- Allows your interface to adapt to unique user intents in real-time rather than relying on fixed, rigid menus
- Minimizes noise by surfacing only the most relevant information and actions exactly when they are required for the task
- Shifted Cognitive Load

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Why does this shift in design philosophy matter for you as a PM? It’s because AI-native UX fundamentally changes the "physics" of how your user interacts with your product. In traditional design, you often force your user to do the heavy lifting—they have to hunt through menus, apply filters, and manually stitch together information.
> Lowered Interaction Costs: AI-native design shifts your user's primary role from a manual "builder" to a strategic "reviewer." By replacing the labor of finding and gathering data with pre-synthesized insights or "Version 0.1" outputs, the interface eliminates the 'blank page' problem. You aren't just making it faster; you are removing the cognitive energy required to start a task.
> Shifted Cognitive Load: You are moving your user from "Instruction" to "Intent." In traditional products, the user's impact is limited by their manual output. By encoding logic directly into the interaction, your system performs high-level reasoning and complex execution, allowing the user to oversee a higher volume of work without a corresponding increase in effort. This is where you scale their judgment.
> Increased System Resilience: Traditional, deterministic UIs are rigid, they break, or feel "clunky" when a user has a non-standard request. An AI-native interface acts as a passive sensor. By capturing implicit signals such as what a user edits, ignores, or accepts, and the system builds a continuous learning loop. This allows the product's interface to become more flexible and personalized without requiring constant engineering tickets for every new edge case.
> Contextual Relevance: Rather than requiring the user to navigate complex, static menus, the system uses intent prediction to surface specific components only when they are needed. This creates a "just-in-time" interface that removes the friction of manual searching. As a PM, your goal is to keep the user in a state of "flow" by hiding complexity until it is contextually necessary.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> The AI feature or product you develop should be invisible by design. Invisible design isn't about making the AI disappear; it's about making the labor disappear. The goal is for AI to blend seamlessly into the user experience, where they don't even notice it's there—yet it’s still working effectively in the background.
> Focus on:
> Using Environmental Signals to Trigger Actions: Stop waiting for a prompt. Design the system to "wake up" based on data events rather than user clicks. You are designing for the event, not the command. If a user uploads a document, the system should already be halfway through the analysis by the time they click "Open."
> Real-World Example: Glean doesn’t wait for you to search; they index your activity in the background so that when you finally do open the app, the "analysis" phase is already complete and the data is ready for instant retrieval.
> Leveraging Intent Prediction to Surface Micro-UIs: Traditional software clutters the screen with every possible tool "just in case." In AI-native design, the UI is dynamic and ephemeral. You only reveal complex controls when the model's confidence in a specific user intent crosses a certain threshold.
> Real-Word Example: Adobe Firefly in Photoshop. The "Generative Fill" bar doesn't sit on the screen at all times. It only appears precisely where your cursor is after you’ve made a selection, predicting that your intent is now to modify that specific area.
> Automating Data Flow Between Tasks: The system should handle the "grunt work" of the product. If Juno generates a roadmap, it shouldn't just be text in a bubble; it should be pre-populated into the roadmap view. You are designing the "glue" that connects features so the user doesn't have to.
> Real-World Example: Salesforce. When the AI identifies a new lead in a transcript, it doesn’t just notify you; it automatically maps that data into the correct CRM fields and drafts the follow-up email. It bridges the gap between "insight" and "action" without the user ever touching a CSV or manual entry form.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Once you’ve defined the behavioral logic for your AI, your next task as a PM is to choose the physical real estate it occupies within your product’s UI.
> Inline & Embedded: This is the most seamless placement. You aren't "going to the AI"; the AI is a property of the data you are already touching. This placement keeps the user in a "flow state" by making the AI a native property of the content itself. Instead of navigating to a tool, the tool exists exactly where the work is happening.
> Real-World Example: Google Docs. The "Help me write" button is inline; it moves as your cursor moves.
> Floating & Contextual: This pattern follows the user’s focus, appearing only when a specific object is selected. It keeps the workspace clean by ensuring advanced controls are invisible until they are contextually relevant to the user's current selection. This is about "Just-in-Time" UI. If the user hasn't selected anything, the UI is 100% empty. The menu only "spawns" when there is an object to act upon.
> Real-World Example: Figma AI. When you click a group of layers, a small floating pill appears at the bottom with options to "Rename" or "Organize."
> Full-Page Canvas & Hubs: However,  sometimes "Invisible" isn't the goal. High-complexity outputs require a dedicated space for review and refinement. When the AI is generating large-scale "V1" drafts, like a full roadmap or a slide deck, you must provide a "Review Room" that allows for deep focus on the final result.
> Real-World Example: Gamma or Canva Magic Design. When these tools generate an entire presentation from a prompt, the UI transitions to a full-screen canvas because the "Value" is the scale of the creation, which requires a dedicated environment for user validation.

### Architecting the AI "Iceberg"

- Traditional mapping focuses on user clicks, screen navigation, and manual inputs.
- Don't just map what the user does; map what the system thinks. The most powerful AI experiences are often the simplest UIs supported by the most complex background logic.
- AI-native mapping focuses on the background orchestration happening between a user’s intent and the final output.
- TOP TIP
- You must decide what remains "Invisible" to keep the experience effortless, and what needs to be "Visible" to build user trust.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Now that we’ve covered the patterns and the pitfalls, let’s talk about how you actually build the "skeleton" of these experiences. We call this Architecting the AI Iceberg. As PMs, you’ll find that the most "magical" products actually have the simplest interfaces. The complexity hasn't disappeared; it has simply moved "underwater" into the backend. This means shifting your focus.
> Traditional mapping is obsessed with where a user clicks. But in an AI-native flow, the most important steps happen between the clicks. You need to account for the background orchestration—how the system interprets a signal, fetches data, and decides on a maneuver—before the user even sees a result.
> Your biggest strategic lever in this phase is deciding what to reveal. If you show too much of the "underwater" logic, you clutter the UX. If you show too little, you lose user trust. You are designing the balance between Invisible Effortlessness and Visible Verification.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Architecting an AI user flow requires you to move away from step-by-step navigation and toward Outcome-Oriented UX. In traditional design, the user holds the map; in AI-native design, the user provides the destination, and the system handles the navigation. This means you are designing for four unique pillars that bridge the gap between the surface and the logic underwater:
> The Trigger: Your goal is to identify the earliest possible "Intent Signal." You are looking for the moment data enters the system, like a calendar invite being sent or a document being uploaded. If you wait for a manual "Start AI" click, you’ve missed the opportunity to provide immediate value.
> The Processing State: This is where you handle the Intelligence Tax. Instead of a generic spinner, use this space to build trust. By showing "Active Synthesis" or breadcrumbs of the AI’s logic, you turn a technical delay into a moment of transparency that justifies the final result.
> The Presentation: Refer back to our Maneuver Mapping. The architecture must decide: is this result a "Starting Point" (Inline), a "Final Answer" (Full-Page), or a "Contextual Layer" (Overlay). The flow should place the output exactly where the user needs to take their next action.
> The Feedback Loop: This is the learning Infrastructure. Every flow must have a sensor. If a user accepts, edits, or rejects the AI's work, that data must flow back to your model. This turns every user interaction into a mini-training session that improves the product's accuracy over time.

> Notes
> The hallucination gap is the primary reason users abandon AI products. Since AI is probabilistic, it will eventually be wrong; your job is to ensure the UI doesn't "lie" to the user when that happens.
> The Problem: When an AI presents a hallucination with the same visual authority as a fact, it destroys the user's mental model of the tool. They stop seeing it as a partner and start seeing it as an unreliable source

### The AI-UX Readiness Checklist

- Before you move to a “magical" invisible UI, you must pass the baseline requirements for trust and functionality. Use this hierarchy to prioritize your design and engineering efforts.
- Level1
- Functional BaselineDoes the AI trigger reliably at the correct moment?
- Define strict confidence thresholds so the AI only automates when it is 90% certain of the user's intent.
- Level2
- Reliability & VerificationCan the user verify the output in under three seconds?Design source scaffolding like inline citations or logic summaries to bridge the trust gap without cluttering the UI.
- Level3
- Magical FlowDoes the interaction remove more labor than it creates? Measure the correction rate. If users are constantly manually fixing the AI’s work, revert from an invisible UI to a validator UI

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> When you move from product strategy to execution, you need a framework to evaluate the quality of your AI-UX. Use the Hierarchy of Needs as your ship criteria to ensure you aren't trying to build "magic" on top of a broken foundation
> Make sure you always start from Level 1:
> Functional (Trigger Accuracy): This is your baseline for technical readiness. You do this by defining strict confidence thresholds. If your "environmental signals" are noisy, meaning the AI triggers at the wrong time, the user will find the automation intrusive rather than helpful.
> Before you ship, or move to Level 2,  you must ensure the "event" and the "action" are perfectly synced.
> Reliable (System Transparency): Once the system is functional, you must bridge the trust gap. Since AI is probabilistic, users are naturally skeptical. You do this by designing "source scaffolding" with elements like hover-states that show the raw data or citations.
> This transparency allows the user to verify the output in seconds rather than re-doing the work themselves and give you the confidence to move to Level 3.
> Magical (Invisible Integration): This is the peak of AI-native design, where the AI feels like a natural extension of the user’s thoughts. You do this by monitoring the "Correction Rate."
> If your data shows users are constantly manual-editing the AI's work, it means your logic isn't ready for invisibility yet, and you should revert back  to a more transparent, Level 2 "Reliable" layer UI.

### Managing the Intelligence Tax

- Unlike traditional software, AI-native products come with intelligence taxes. These represent inherent technical trade-offs in speed and data handling that must be managed through the UI.
- Users are hesitant to share proprietary or personal data with a model they don't own. To manage this, build permission-first architecture with clear privacy badges and implement opt-In memory features that give the user surgical control over what the AI remembers.
- 2
- The Privacy Tax
- High-quality model inference takes time, creating a "dead zone" where the user thinks the app is broken. To manage this latency, use streaming text responses or status breadcrumbs (e.g., "Scanning backlog...") to turn idle time into active progress.
- 1
- The Latency Tax

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> As a PM, you have to manage the "Intelligence Tax." These aren't bugs; they are inherent properties of working with Large Language Models. Your job is to use the UI to mask these technical limitations.
> The Latency Tax: Manage this by focusing on Perceived Performance. If an AI takes 5 seconds to respond, that feels like an eternity in a static UI. But if the UI "streams" the text character-by-character, or shows a status bar saying "Reading your PDF...", the user perceives the system as being hard at work for them. You are turning a wait into a value-add.
> The Privacy Tax: Data anxiety is a major barrier to retention, so make your product loudly private. Don't hide your data policy in the footer. Use permission-first design and ask the user, "Can I remember this for next time” with a clear privacy shield icon when the AI is working in a secure environment.
> When you manage these two taxes correctly, you move your product from "Experimental" to "Enterprise-Ready." You are proving to the user that your product is both fast enough to use and safe enough to trust with their best ideas.
