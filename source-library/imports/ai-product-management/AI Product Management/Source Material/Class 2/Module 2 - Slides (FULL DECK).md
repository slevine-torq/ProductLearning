### These are AI features that look impressive in a demo but provide little utility. They often feel like AI has been slapped on to a product without respecting the specific user workflow, often resulting in features that users ignore after the novelty wears off.

- “Shiny Distraction” Features
- 🪄 Fake Good
- EXAMPLES
- Generic chatbots on every screen that hallucinate or provide circular answers; AI summary tools that simply add more word to a document without clarity or synthesis.
- These are silent, high-reliability AI features that solve unglamorous problems. They focus on removing friction, reducing human error, and accelerating repetitive tasks, providing deep leverage that becomes essential to the product experience.
- “Workflow Engine” Features
- ⚙️ Boring Killer
- EXAMPLES
- Automatic reconciliation logic that silently matches support tickets to CRM data; A specialized copilot that reliably turns messy interview notes into high-fidelity PRD drafts.
- VS

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Establishing an AI-first mindset requires you to develop a critical eye for where/when artificial intelligence actually belongs in your product. It’s easy to get swept up in the hype of what looks "cool" in a high-speed demo, but your job as a PM is to differentiate between surface-level novelties and deep architectural leverage.
> There are two paths: "Fake Good" and "Boring Killers:"
> Fake Good: You’re looking at shiny distractions. These are often generic implementations, like an AI chat bubble slapped onto every corner of your UI, that haven't been designed with the actual user workflow in mind. They might look impressive to a stakeholder for five minutes, but because they often hallucinate or provide circular summaries that don't add real synthesis, users quickly lose trust and stop using them. These features add words, but they don't add clarity.
> Boring Killers: These are the silent engines that make your product indispensable. These features aren't unsexy; they are high-reliability tools that solve unglamorous problems like manual data reconciliation or turning a mess of discovery notes into a structured PRD draft. They focus on reducing cycle time and eliminating human error.
> As a strategist, you aren't looking for a magic wand; you are looking for the gears that automate the invisible, repetitive friction in your user’s day. If you can’t map your AI feature to this kind of concrete leverage, it probably shouldn't be built.

## Slide 9

### The Three-Layer Model

- 01 User Workflow
- 02 Technical AI Solution
- 03 Business Outcome
- Identify the specific Job-to-be-Done or friction point in the user's current journey.
- Example: A PM needs to synthesize 10 user interviews into a strategy doc.
- Define the macro goal this bet moves, such as increasing revenue, reducing cost, or mitigating risk.
- Example: Reduce discovery cycle time from 5 days to 4 hours per feature.
- Determine which technical solution (LLM, RAG, Agentic workflow, or Multimodal) best addresses that specific step.
- Example: Multimodal RAG to process interview transcripts and video screenshots.
- To turn a value prop into a tangible build, you must align three distinct layers to get a green light.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> This three-layer model is your central mental model as an AI PM. It is designed to stop you from "solutioning"—falling in love with a technical capability like a new multimodal model and looking for a place to stick it in your UI. This framework forces you to work from the outside in to ensure every feature you build has a purpose.
> Start at the top: User Workflow layer. To make this effective, you need to be extremely specific: who is the actor, what is the trigger, and what are the current pain points? For example, instead of saying "AI for research," define the workflow as "A PM needs to synthesize 10 user interviews into a strategy doc." You have to identify a high-friction moment in the current journey before you ever mention the tech.
> Move to the middle: AI Capability layer. This is where you determine the minimum AI capability required to solve that specific problem. Do you just need a plain LLM for text transformation? Do you need RAG because the task requires trustworthy, up‑to‑date internal company knowledge? Or is it an Agentic task that requires multi-step action taking? By matching the capability to the workflow, you avoid over-engineering. In the interviews to strategy example, you might choose a Multimodal RAG setup. You need Multimodal capabilities to "read" screenshots shared during the calls, and RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) to ensure the AI only uses the specific data from those transcripts rather than hallucinating general industry trends.
> Finish with the bottom layer: Business Outcome. As a PM, you need to tie your AI bet to hard numbers—whether that is time saved per week, a reduction in cycle time from idea to ship, or moving the needle on NPS. For every opportunity you identify today, you should be able to complete this sentence: "In this workflow, using [capability] should move [metric] by X." Finishing with our example, success of this bet is measured by cycle time reduction. By automating the synthesis, you reduce the time from "Research Complete" to "Strategy Drafted" by 80%, allowing the product team to move from monthly to weekly discovery cycles.
> If you can’t draw a straight line through all three layers, you aren't building a product strategy; you’re just experimenting with technology.

## Slide 11

### Choosing Your Level of Autonomy

- Assist
- Agent
- Copilot
- Reactive: Provides suggestions and nudges; user drives every step.
- Semi-autonomous: Drafts content and proposes actions; user approves
- Autonomous: Takes action and executes tasks end-to-end with guardrails
- Minimal: Limited to a single interface or tool (e.g., search bar, text editor).
- Focused: Connects to specific data sources to perform workflow tasks.
- Broad: Orchestrates multiple tools and systems to complete a goal.
- A PM types a feature name and the AI suggests three relevant tags based on the roadmap.
- A PM requests a PRD draft; the AI pulls data from Jira to write it for user review.
- A PM sets a goal; the AI gathers data, drafts the spec, and updates the project tracker.
- EXAMPLE
- INTEGRATION
- AUTONOMY
- ALWAYS ASK: “What is the safest useful level here for this product?”

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> As an AI PM, one of your most critical decisions is determining how much control you give the model versus how much you leave with the human. This is fundamental strategic decision that balances product value against the risks inherent in probabilistic systems.
> These can be categorized this into three levels: Assist, Copilot, and Agent
> Assist: The human is completely in the driver's seat. The AI provides suggestions or simple nudges—think of a predictive search bar or a tag suggester. This is the safest way to provide value. It’s your go-to starting point when the risk of a model hallucination is high and the user needs to maintain full accountability for the data.
> Copilot: The AI starts to take action by drafting content or proposing multi-step workflows, but it still requires the human to review and approve. This is where you’ll see those "10x speed gains." By pulling from internal data to draft a PRD, you've done the heavy lifting, but you've kept a "human in the loop" to ensure accuracy before anything is committed.
> Agents: The AI handles end-to-end tasks with pre-defined guardrails. The human sets the goal, and the model orchestrates the tools and triggers to finish the job—like updating your CRM and project management tools simultaneously.
> Your primary question as a PM should always be: "What is the safest useful level here?" You don't always need an Agent to solve a problem; sometimes, a well-placed Assist is exactly what the user needs to stay in their flow

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> As any PM knows, your job is all about balance. With AI products, you are constantly balancing the desire for "Magic" with the necessity of "Safety." This matrix is your primary tool for deciding which level of autonomy is appropriate for a specific feature. It stops the team from falling into the trap of trying to build an autonomous agent for a task where the organization simply cannot afford a mistake.
> To make the strategic choice, evaluate every feature idea by the cost of failure versus the scale of the benefit. Start from a specific job‑to‑be‑done (e.g., “create monthly exec roadmap update”) and ask: How risky is it if the AI is wrong? How impactful if we get it right? Given that, what autonomy level is appropriate?
> Here are four examples to make the concept clearer:
> High Impact / Low Risk: Synthesizing 100+ raw feedback snippets into themes.This is the "Gold Mine" for AI automation. The impact is high because it saves a PM hours of manual tagging, allowing for faster discovery cycles. However, the risk is low because these themes are used for internal directional research, not for immediate production changes. If the AI misinterprets one or two snippets or creates a redundant theme, it’s a minor nuisance, not a product failure. Because the "Cost of Failure" is so low compared to the "Value of Discovery," this is a prime candidate for an Agentic workflow that works autonomously in the background.
> High Impact / High Risk: Auto-prioritizing roadmap and changing live dates. This is the most dangerous quadrant for an AI PM. The efficiency gain of an AI perfectly balancing engineering velocity and business goals would be massive—it’s the ultimate "Boring Killer." But the risk is existential to your team culture. If a probabilistic model makes a "best guess" and shifts a shipping date without context, you lose the trust of every stakeholder and engineer involved. Because the impact is so high but the cost of a hallucination is catastrophic, we keep this at the Assist level. The AI can present a "Suggested Roadmap," but the human must be the one to click "Apply."
> Low Impact / Low Risk: Drafting routine stakeholder update emails.These are your "Quick Wins." Drafting status updates is a repetitive friction point that every PM faces. While the impact isn't "company-changing," it improves the PM's quality of life. The risk is negligible because there is a standard "Human-in-the-Loop" step—the PM has to review the draft before hitting send. Since the user is already naturally positioned to catch errors, a Copilot experience is the perfect fit. It removes the "blank page" problem while keeping the user's signature as the final seal of approval.
> Low Impact / High Risk: Automated deletion of "inactive" user accounts.This is the "Trap" quadrant. You might feel pressure to "clean up data" using AI, but the trade-off is completely lopsided. The business value of automating account deletion is minimal compared to the risk of an AI model accidentally wiping the data of a high-value customer who was simply "quiet" for a few months. When the cost of a hallucination is permanent data loss and the upside is just minor database hygiene, the strategic decision is simple: Do not build. Keep this as a deterministic, code-based rule, or keep it manual. It’s not worth the risk of a probabilistic error.

### Responsible AI:Your Strategic Guardrails

- 🤝 Human OversightThe “Safety Valve”
- 🔍 TransparencyThe “Disclosure Label”
- 👨‍⚖️ Bias AwarenessThe “Equity Filter”
- 🔒 PrivacyThe “Digital Vault”
- AI must support decisions rather than finalize them, as unmonitored autonomy can lead to catastrophic outcomes.
- Clearly distinguish between human-verified and AI-generated content as hidden AI outputs destroy brand credibility when errors occur.
- Actively audit training data to ensure it doesn't amplify historical imbalances as biased models lead to legal and PR crises.
- Use only approved, secure environments for proprietary data as public LLMs are not private; accidental leaks become permanent.
- REAL-WORLD STAKES:In 2023, a Belgian man tragically took his life after following advice from an unmonitored AI chatbot during a conversation about climate anxiety.
- REAL-WORLD STAKES:CNET published AI-generated financial articles with factual errors, damaging their brand reputation once the lack of disclosure was revealed.
- REAL-WORLD STAKES:Amazon scrapped an AI recruitment tool after it was found to penalize resumes with the word “women’s,” favoring male-coded language.
- REAL-WORLD STAKES:Samsung employees leaked trade secrets by pasting confidential source code into a public LLM, making the data part of the global model.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Governance isn't an academic exercise or a "Legal" problem; it is a core component of your Product Strategy. These four guardrails determine the viability of an AI opportunity. If you don't bake these into your strategy now, you aren't building a product—you're building a corporate liability.
> Human Oversight (The Safety Valve): As a PM, you must decide where the AI's job ends and the human's begins. Strategy is about accountability; you cannot delegate the "onus" of your product’s decisions to a model.
> Example: In 2023, a Belgian man tragically took his life after following advice from an unmonitored AI chatbot during a conversation about climate anxiety
> Transparency (The Disclosure Label): Trust is a strategic asset. If users can't distinguish between a human-verified fact and an AI-generated guess, your product's value proposition collapses the moment the model hallucinates.
> Example: CNET used AI to write financial articles — but didn’t disclose it until errors were discovered. The articles had factual mistakes and were quietly edited, leading to criticism about lack of transparency.
> Bias Awareness (The Equity Filter): AI is a mirror of your training data. If your data reflects historical imbalances, your AI will scale those imbalances. Strategic governance means auditing your "inputs" to ensure you aren't unintentionally narrowing your market or creating a PR crisis.
> Example: Amazon’s AI recruitment tool, which aimed to rapidly screen resumes and pull out the top candidates to be considered, was biased against women. The computer models were trained to vet applicants by observing patterns in resumes submitted to the company over a 10-year period. Most came from men, a reflection of male dominance across the tech industry, so the model trained itself that male candidates were therefore preferable, penalizing any resumes with women’s names.
> Data Privacy (The Digital Vault): Data is your moat, but only if you keep it. In the strategy phase, you must define the "vault"—deciding which proprietary assets are strictly prohibited from leaving your secure infrastructure.
> Example: Samsung employees accidentally leaked source code to ChatGPT. They pasted confidential code into ChatGPT to debug it, unintentionally exposing trade secrets.
> Now that we have the Principles for a responsible strategy, we move to the Audit. Let’s look at the four "Gates" that determine if your organization will actually let you move this opportunity into development.

> The biggest mistake PMs make is treating AI as a "blue-sky" brainstorming session. In reality, you are often navigating intense market noise and leadership teams that are rightfully wary of "magic" features that fail to move the needle.
> This requires a reframing, which you can do with the help of a AI Strategy One-Pager. How?
> The Executive Test: Treat this one-pager as the document you send to a stakeholder looking for a reason to say "no." Your job is to make the rationale undeniable and the data the primary driver of the conversation.
> Validation over Inspiration: This isn't about what AI could do; it’s about what it must do to solve a specific, high-value bottleneck.
> The Survival Filter: Shipping the wrong AI feature is significantly more expensive than traditional software due to the irreversible one way doors we saw earlier (like data exposure or habitual lock-in).If you can’t articulate the friction and the outcome on a single page, the project isn't ready for a production roadmap.
> So what should you include in your one-pager to get the buy-in you need?
