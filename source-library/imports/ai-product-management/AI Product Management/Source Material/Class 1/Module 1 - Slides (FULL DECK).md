**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Due to the nature of AI, this is arguably the single biggest mental model shift for a PM in this era: the move from predictable to probabilistic systems.
> For most of your career, you have likely managed deterministic systems. Think about a standard search filter or a login authentication flow. If a user filters a database for 'Active Accounts' created in '2024', the SQL query runs and returns the exact same list of rows, every single time. That 100% predictability is the foundation of how we write acceptance criteria and unit tests.
> Modern AI, however, is non-deterministic by design. As we see with tools like ChatGPT or features like AI-generated meeting summaries, the system is built for variability. You can feed an LLM the exact same meeting transcript five times, and it might highlight different action items or change the tone of the summary each time.
> This brings us to the critical takeaway: You can no longer write a requirement that says 'The output will be X.'Your job now is to define the qualities and guardrails of an acceptable output. You are no longer specifying the result; you are managing the range of possibilities. This requires a completely new way of thinking.

## Slide 16

### Ambiguity creates inconsistency. To ensure predictable model performance, every prompt must explicitly define these four dimensions.

- FORMATDefines the output structure.“Return a markdown table with headers”
- CONSTRAINTSDefines the guardrails.“Do not speculate. Use only the provided transcript”
- ROLEDefines the model's expertise.“Act as a senior B2B product researcher”
- TASKDefines the specific goal.“Synthesize insights from raw feedback”
- The Anatomy of a High-Quality Prompt
- Model Configuration
- 🦾

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> This framework isn't just a 'hack'—it is the standard configuration pattern for reliable instructions. The difference between a simple question and a production-grade prompt is the difference between a generic answer and a strategic, consistent output. This connects directly to the 'Probabilistic' nature of AI we discussed earlier: Structure reduces randomness.
> Every reliable prompt must define these four keys:
> Role (The Persona): Tell the model who to be. By setting 'Act as a Senior Researcher,' you aren't just roleplaying; you are forcing the model to access a specific subset of its training data, using more technical vocabulary and logic than a generic assistant would.
> Task (The Verb): Give it a single, actionable goal. 'Analyze,' 'Summarize,' 'Transform.' Ambiguity here leads to wandering answers.
> Constraints (The Guardrails): Note the shift here. We used to call this just 'Context,' but for you, it is Constraints. This is where you limit the risk. Don't just give it the transcript; tell it what not to do. 'Do not speculate outside this text.' 'Do not use sales jargon.' This is your primary defense against hallucination.
> Format (The Schema): Specify the exact output structure. If you need a table, ask for Markdown. If you need a list of insights, ask for bullet points. Think of this as defining the "shape" of the information before it hits the screen.
> This structure is the baseline. If you miss one of these four, you are introducing inconsistency into your product.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> In Lab 1, you were working exclusively in the Middle Layer—writing user prompts within the constraints that Lovable had already built for you. Because Lovable’s own "System Messages" are so well-engineered, the product worked exactly as intended to build your UI. But when you build your own AI-powered features, you can't rely on someone else's guardrails. To build a predictable product, you have to go deeper.
> Here you can see how prompts are not tricks or magic. They are configuration tools very similar to writing UX guidelines or defining API behavior.  This is exactly how Airbnb sets tone rules for its listing generation tools, how Salesforce structures support assistant prompts, and how Spotify tunes summarization prompts for creator insights.Think of this stack as your new PRD. The System Messages Layer is your requirements doc—it's where you lock in the rules. If you don't configure this layer, you are leaving your product's behavior up to chance.
> System Messages: Think of this as the 'Job Description' or the 'Constitution.' It defines the global rules—tone, safety, and format—that apply to every interaction, hidden from the user.
> User Prompts: This is the variable. It’s the specific intent your user expresses in the moment when interacting with your AI-powered feature.
> Tools & Functions: This is the newest surface. It defines not what the model says, but what it can do—like calling an API or looking up data.
> If you don't configure all three, you aren't building a product; you're just wrapping a chat box. So, how do you actually configure that System Layer to ensure the 'Brain' is as high-fidelity as the 'Face'? Let’s check out the core prompting strategies.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> To turn your "Dumb UI" into a smart assistant, you have to move beyond basic instructions and start configuring the system's underlying logic.
> Now that you understand the anatomy of a prompt, let's look at your strategic options. As a PM, you aren't just 'typing into a box'; you are choosing a specific configuration based on the problem you need to solve. You have three main prompt engineering strategies to deploy:
> Zero-Shot - The Baseline: This is where everyone starts. You just give instructions. It is excellent for speed and ideation—like generating 50 ideas for a roadmap. However, it has high variance. You never know exactly what format you'll get back
> Few-Shot - The Standardizer: This is your most important tool for production consistency. If you need the AI to write Jira tickets or format JSON data, simply 'asking' often fails. Instead, you provide 2 or 3 examples of 'perfect' output inside the prompt. It’s 'Monkey See, Monkey Do.' This ensures the AI mimics your team’s exact standards every single time.
> Chain-of-Thought - The Analyst: Finally, we have the logic solver. Remember, LLMs are probabilistic, not logical. If you ask a complex market sizing question, they often guess. By forcing the model to 'Think step-by-step,' you force it to generate a reasoning path before the final answer. This drastically reduces errors in calculation and strategy.
> Let’s look at each in more detail.

### The Prompt:

- The Result:
- Output 1: ‘The reports load slowly’ is a bug.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Let's look at Few-Shot Prompting in practice. First, a quick clarification: let’s be clear that Zero-Shot isn't 'bad.' In fact, it's excellent for creativity, brainstorming, and drafting from a blank page.
> But it is often the wrong tool for the specific job of product consistency. Look at the example on the left:
> If you use a Zero-Shot prompt to 'Classify feedback,' you are asking the model to guess your internal definitions. To the AI, 'Slow login' might look like a bug. But to your Engineering team, that might be a performance story (a Feature Request). If you try to fix this by writing paragraphs of instructions, you are fighting the model.
> Instead, look at the right. We solve it with Few-Shot. We simply give it an example: 'Slow login → Feature Request.' Now, when the model sees 'Slow reports,' it doesn't need to guess. It sees the pattern and mimics it.
> The takeaway: Zero-Shot is for when you want the AI to be creative. Few-Shot is for when you want the AI to be compliant.

## Slide 26

### Chain-of Thought (COT) Prompting

- The Prompt:
- “Is ‘Add AI Avatars’ a high priority feature? Think step-by-step:
- 1. Define the primary driver of our Churn.
- 2. Evaluate if ‘AI Avatars’ directly solves that driver.
- 3. Assign priority based only on that evaluation.”
- The Result:
- “Step 1: Churn is primarily driven by high costs. Step 2: AI Avatars add value but do not reduce cost. Conclusion: Priority Low.”
- Pattern matching isn't enough for logic. You need reasoning.
- The Prompt:
- “Is ‘Add AI Avatars’ a high priority feature? (Context: Our Q3 Goal is reducing Churn)”
- The Result:
- “Priority: High. AI features are trending and usually drive user engagement.”
- The Failure:
- Even if you provide examples of other "High Priority" features, the model relies on surface-level patterns. It sees "AI" (a popular topic) and predicts "High Priority," ignoring the specific logic of your goal.
- Prediction Only
- Prediction + Reasoning

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Now, why do we need a second technique? Why isn't 'Few-Shot' enough? On the previous slide, we saw that Few-Shot is perfect for Style. If you give the model 3 examples of a bug report, it learns the format perfectly.
> But here is the trap: Few-Shot is terrible at Logic. If you give the model 3 examples of 'High Priority' features, it just learns that 'Cool Tech = High Priority.' It stops thinking.
> In the example on the left, we asked about 'AI Avatars.' Without CoT: The model gets excited. It sees 'AI', matches it to its internal pattern of 'Good Feature', and says High Priority. It completely ignores our goal of 'Reducing Churn'.
> We fix this by adding reasoning with chain-of-thought. By adding the magic phrase 'Think step-by-step,' we force it to slow down and make it 'show its work.' By forcing it to explicitly link the feature to the goal first, it realizes the logic doesn't hold up.
> The Rule: Use Few-Shot for formatting (JSON, Style). Use Chain-of-Thought for analysis (Math, Logic, Priority)."

### From Strategy to Spec

- Model Configuration
- Hyperparameters
- Adjusts response randomness to control creativity or precision:
- Low (0.2): Product Logic/Code/Data
- High (0.7+): Ideation/Chat
- Limits the model to only top % of likely words to control quality vocabulary:
- Use this to cut off "weird" or "long-tail" hallucinations without making the model boring or lowering temperature.
- Sets hard limit on token output to control latency and cost:
- Always set a limit to prevent the model from rambling, getting stuck in loops, or wasting API budget.
- The Budget Knob:Max Tokens
- The Risk Slider:Temperature
- The Quality Bar:Top P
- Your prompt is the software.         These settings are the operating environment.
- Once you’ve defined your system prompt, you must define the physics of the model environment.

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> We have covered the text of the prompt. Now we need to look at the settings. Hyperparameters let you tune the safety, creativity, and predictability of your product.
> Temperature: This is your creativity slider and your most important knob. Lower temperature gives you precise facts; higher temperature gives you surprises.
> For example: Spotify uses low temperature for support summaries because they need exact facts, not creativity.
> Netflix, on the other hand, uses high temperature for title brainstorming because they want wild ideas.
> Airbnb sits in the middle to keep natural language suggestions feeling friendly but safe.
> Max Length & Penalties:This is your  cost & quality knob that determines constraints by setting a hard limit on the max tokens a model can output. Max Tokens control cost and speed, while penalties help reduce repetition.
> For example: Spotify uses this to force concise summaries.
> Salesforce uses it to compress CRM notes.
> Figma uses penalties this to ensure UI copy generators don't just print the same label five times in a row.
> Top P: This is your vocabulary filter to controls how 'wide' or 'narrow' the model thinks by limiting the model to a specific percent of likely words.
> For example: Salesforce uses lower values for legal content to ensure safety and repeatability.
> Airbnb uses it to enforce trust and safety messaging.
> Netflix expands it to allow for diverse, creative exploration in storytelling.
> The Pro-Tip: Parameter Sweeps Don't just guess these numbers. Do what the pros do: run a Parameter Sweep. Instead of guessing what works, test multiple settings and pick the one that aligns with your product goals. This is exactly how Netflix optimizes assistant tone and how Spotify tunes those playlist descriptions you see every day.

## Slide 29

### Don't use fine-tuning to teach the model your proprietary data or policies. Models are probabilistic engines, not databases; they cannot "look up" facts and will often hallucinate facts they aren't 100% sure of. To stop hallucinations regarding data, you will need Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG).

- Fine-tuning "bakes" instructions directly into the model's weights.
- Unlike Few-Shot Prompting, this eliminates the need to send long, expensive examples with every API call, reducing latency and cost.
- Behaviour, Not Knowledge
- The Role of Fine-Tuning
- The Knowledge Misconception
- Github Copilot Example: Copilot isn't just "prompted" to write code. It is fine-tuned on billions of lines of code to instinctively understand syntax, style, and logic patterns without needing specific instructions.
- Pro Tip: Use fine-tuning to bake in skills, not facts!

**Speaker notes:**

> Notes
> Perhaps you are thinking: 'Wait, didn't you just tell us Few-Shot Prompting is for style? Why do I need Fine-Tuning?”
> Here is the difference: Scale and Latency.
> If you use Few-Shot Prompting, you have to feed the model 10 examples of your brand voice every single time a user sends a message. That eats up tokens (money) and slows down the response time.
> Fine-Tuning solves this. By training the model on those examples once, you 'bake' that behavior into its brain. It becomes cheaper: You stop paying for those example tokens on every call. Plus, it becomes faster: The model just 'knows' what to do without reading a long preamble.
> Tip: Use Fine-Tuning to bake in Skills (Code, Style, Format). Never use it to bake in Facts (Prices, Policies). If your prices change, you don't want to have to re-train your brain surgery just to update a number.

### “The model gives inconsistent answers”

- If the model oscillates between good and bad outputs, it usually just needs clearer guardrails or examples in the prompt. This is the fastest, cheapest fix.
- "The model doesn't know our specific data."
- If the user asks about "User X’s refund status," the model can't guess that. It needs to read your database. No amount of training will fix this; it needs access.
- "The model sounds like a robot, not our brand."
- If the content is correct but the vibe is wrong (e.g., too formal, wrong code syntax), you need to retrain the model's weights to instinctively mimic your specific voice or format.

> Notes
> As an AI PM, your stakeholders will often come to you shouting, 'The AI is broken!' or 'We need to fine-tune a model immediately!’ Your job is not to just say yes. Your job is to diagnose the root cause so you pick the right architectural tool. Think of this as your triage framework:
> The Consistency Problem (Few-Shot Prompting): First, check for inconsistency. If the model answers perfectly one time and fails the next, or if it hallucinates wildly, you rarely need a new model. You usually just need better instructions. Always start with Few-Shot Prompting. It’s the cheapest, fastest lever you have. If you can fix it with 3 examples in the prompt, do not spend $50k training a custom model.
> The Knowledge Problem (RAG): Next, the big one: Missing Data. If a user asks, 'Where is my refund?', no amount of fine-tuning will help the model know that answer. That data lives in your SQL database, not in the model's brain. For this, you need RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation). This connects the brain (AI) to the book (Your Database).
> The Style Problem (Fine-Tuning): Finally, look at the behavior. If the model gives the correct answer but sounds like a generic robot, or if it writes code that works but violates your engineering style guide, that is a behavior gap. This is where Fine-Tuning shines. You use it to 'bake' your brand voice or strict formatting rules directly into the model's weights so it feels native to your product.
