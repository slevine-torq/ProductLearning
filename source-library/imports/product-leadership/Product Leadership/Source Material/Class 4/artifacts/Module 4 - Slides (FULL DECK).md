## Section 5 — SECTION BREAK 01: Understand Stakeholders and Their Stakes

> **Alignment is built before the room, not in it.** Read who actually holds power, understand what each stakeholder is protecting, and diagnose why a specific person is not moving before you try to move them.

---

## Section 6 — Quick vote: the unmoved executive · ACTIVITY · 5 min

*You have a strategy you believe in, but a key executive is not convinced. What do you do first?*
**A.** Go back and strengthen your argument with more data before the next meeting.
**B.** Have a one-on-one conversation with them before the next meeting.

**Where it lands:** Both matter, and the order depends on *why* they aren't convinced. **If the executive already has the information and is still not moved, more data will not shift them.** What they need is a conversation that surfaces what they are protecting, what your yes costs them, and what would make them a sponsor rather than a skeptic.

---

## Section 7 — The org chart is not the power map · FRAMEWORK

Titles tell you who is accountable, not how decisions actually get made.

| | **Formal Power** | **Informal Power** |
|---|---|---|
| *What it is* | The power you can see on paper | Influence from trust, history, and relationships |
| *Questions* | Who holds the title and the budget line? Who signs off officially? | Whose opinion does the decider check before committing? Who can quietly stall a decision without ever saying no? |
| **Impact** | Tells you **who needs to say yes** for a decision to stick | Tells you **whose support you need to earn before you walk into the room** |

---

## Section 8 — Stakeholder mapping: who decides, who influences, who blocks · FRAMEWORK

Axes: Influence (low → high) × Agreement (resistant → aligned). **The map is only useful if it changes what you do next.**

| | Resistant | Aligned |
|---|---|---|
| **High influence** | **Blockers** — Find out what they are protecting before you try to move them. Addressing that directly is what actually shifts them. | **Sponsors** — Their value is in their voice as much as their vote. Give them your narrative and your specific ask so they can advocate in rooms you are not in. |
| **Low influence** | **Noise to Manage** — Concerns are real but should not set the agenda or slow the decision. Acknowledge and address briefly. | **Validators** — Their signal validates your case and makes it easier for senior sponsors to back you. Channel their support upward. |

---

## Section 9 — Three types of resistance · FRAMEWORK

The map tells you *where* each person sits. It cannot tell you *why* a specific person is not moving — and without that diagnosis, every influence move is a guess.

| Type | What it means | What moves them | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Logical** | They do not believe your argument is correct | Better evidence, clearer reasoning, a proof point | The executive pushing back on your roadmap may simply not yet see how it connects to the company's Q3 revenue goal |
| **Interest** | Your proposal costs them something real | Find where your interests converge; show how your ask serves their goal too | The CPO who won't approve your migration may believe the logic, but the cost is 6 months of disruption to their eng org |
| **Identity** | Your proposal challenges a decision they are known for | Frame your ask as the next chapter, not a criticism of the last one | The architect whose system you are replacing built it from the ground up; replacing it feels like erasing their contribution |

**The most common mistake is treating interest or identity resistance as a logical problem, which makes it worse.**

## Section 10 — The Five Whys diagnosis · FRAMEWORK

Ask why five times. Each peels back one layer until you reach something you can actually address. **Stop when the answer shifts from what they said to what you missed.**

| The Why | What it probes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Why aren't they approving it? | The stated objection | *The CPO is not convinced the ROI justifies the disruption.* |
| Why aren't they convinced? | Whether the stated reason holds | *Our model does not connect to their Q3 OKR on revenue growth.* |
| Why doesn't it connect? | The framing assumption underneath | *We framed the ask as cost reduction, not a growth enabler.* |
| Why did we frame it that way? | Where preparation broke down | *We built the case around our own priorities, not theirs.* |
| Why didn't we ask? | The root cause | *We walked in assuming we already knew what the CPO cared about. We were wrong.* |

> 📌 **The fifth why almost always exposes a framing problem, not a fundamental disagreement about the idea itself.**

## Section 11 — SECTION BREAK 02: Making and Defending Hard Calls

> The hardest part of leading is rarely the calls with a clear answer. It is the ones where every path costs something and you are still the one who has to choose, and then own the outcome.

---

## Section 12 — The calls that land on you

Three recur often enough to recognize before you're in them:
- **Whose work gets cut** — You will fund one team's bet by starving another's, and both will have made a reasonable case.
- **When to overrule** — You will override a decision your team made, knowing they will not agree.
- **When you're overruled** — You will be handed a decision you disagree with, and have to back it as your own.

---

## Section 13 — The hidden costs your instinct skips · FRAMEWORK

Your gut is fast and earned, but quiet about second-order costs. Three feed **upward** into judgment — they inform the call without ever making it for you.

Structure: *The Judgment Call* ← *Instinct + Experience* ← three inputs:

| Input | The cost your instinct skips |
|---|---|
| **The Organization** | Every yes sets a precedent and shifts resourcing, which signals what the org will reward next |
| **Relationships** | A call that looks fair from the outside can still cost you a peer's confidence if they were not brought in |
| **The Business** | What the trade does to revenue, speed, or risk — often in ways the upside hides until later |

---

## Section 14 — What no one warns you about hard calls

Five things with no framework, learned only by living them:
- Your team reads your **conviction**, not your private doubts.
- You move **further** from the decision, not closer, the more senior you get.
- The calls that need the most thought get the **least time** to make them.
- The goal is not being right, but **owning the outcome either way**.
- **No one shares the weight** of the decision with you, even when everyone has an opinion on it.

---

## Section 15 — How to say no so it holds · FRAMEWORK

> A soft no buys you a comfortable meeting and a decision that comes back next week wearing a different hat.

**The test before you deliver a no:** could the person repeat this back as a clear no, or could they walk away thinking it was a maybe? If there is room to hear a maybe, tighten it until there is only one way to read it.

1. **Lead with what stays open, not what's closing** — A no that only names what you are closing reads as a rejection. Point to the path still available and the same decision lands as a redirection.
2. **State it plainly enough to survive being repeated** — Most nos get reopened because the person is not sure they heard one.
3. **Hold firm on the call, stay generous with the person** — The warmth belongs in how you carry the person, not in the decision. Softening the call is what creates the ambiguity.
4. **Offer the path forward in the same breath as the no** — Separating them leaves a gap the person fills with disappointment. Together, a refusal becomes a trade they can accept.

## Section 16 — LAB (Exercise 1) · 15 min · Write your alignment communication

Take a hard decision you already made and write the communication that gets a **specific stakeholder** aligned on it. *This is a communication to a person, not a justification of the strategy.*

1. **Write the no** — take one Hard No from your M2 backlog, address it to your M3 stakeholder
2. **Name what stays open** — acknowledge their legitimate need, then offer the path forward in the same breath
3. **Read it back** — could they walk away thinking it was a maybe? Tighten until there's only one reading

## Section 19 — SECTION BREAK 03: Driving Alignment Through Negotiation Tactics

> You have made the call and know how to hold it. Now bring the people around you with you.

---

## Section 20 — Lead with your interest, not your problem · FRAMEWORK

**Positions are zero-sum**: when two people both need the same resource, someone loses. Go underneath *what* you are asking for to *why* — two people with conflicting positions often share the same interest, and that is where a deal exists.

| | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | *"I need three engineers pulled from your team for Q3."* | Leading with what you want creates a zero-sum conversation |
| **Interest** | *"We both need the AI layer stable before the board review. How do we get there together?"* | Reframes competing demands into a shared challenge |
| **BATNA** | *"If we cannot align, I propose a shared sprint with two engineers from each team."* | Know your alternative beforehand so you don't agree to something worse simply because you weren't prepared |

---

## Section 21 — The executive narrative · FRAMEWORK

The same thinking your team knows, retold for someone who has **ninety seconds** and a dozen other decisions to weigh. Three components:

- **The Recommendation** — the call you're asking them to back, stated plainly enough that they could repeat it to someone else without you in the room.
- **The Trade-Off** — what the bet itself gives up, **named by you**. Every real recommendation closes one door to open another, and stating that cost plainly is what separates a decision from a sales pitch.
- **The Reason to Trust It** — the data point, precedent, or early signal. It turns your judgment into something they can stand behind.

---

## Section 22 — BLUF: put the ask first, not last · FRAMEWORK

> **The single most common reason a strong case fails is not the thinking behind it, but where the ask sits.**

**❌ Buried in context** — three paragraphs of history, cost analysis, and competitive context, *then* "Given all of this, I believe we should consider a 6-month infrastructure consolidation project before Q4." → Executive reads three paragraphs before the point, and likely stops before reaching it.

**✅ Bottom Line Up Front:**
> *"I'm asking you to approve a 6-month infrastructure consolidation. It will reduce deployment complexity by 60% and unblock three roadmap items currently stalled."* **← ask in 1st sentence**
> The cost is $340K and one quarter of reduced feature velocity.
> The cost of not doing it: another 18 months of the same bottleneck, ~$180K in annual maintenance overhead, growing retention risk among senior engineers.
> The one question I expect: "Why now?" Answer: three competitors consolidated their platforms in H1. We're building on sand.

The BLUF version also **names the trade-off and answers the expected objection before it is raised**, which signals you have already done the thinking from the executive's side of the table.

---

## Section 23 — Deliver the narrative

| Format | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The Pre-Read** | Before the meeting | A one-page document sent ahead so the room arrives already knowing your recommendation. It must carry the case without you there, so everything that doesn't change the decision comes out. |
| **The 3-Min Ask** | In the meeting | The spoken version for when you have the floor and not a second more. Open with the recommendation, name the one trade-off that matters, answer the question you know is coming before they raise it. |

---

## Section 24 — The negotiation sequence · FRAMEWORK

> **Most conflicts are two people describing the same goal in different languages.**

1. **Before · come prepared** — Write down what you actually *need* versus what you're *asking for*. They are rarely the same. Know your BATNA. *"I need the AI layer locked" vs. "I need 8 clean weeks of certainty on the schema."*
2. **Opening · observe then ask** — Lead with what you observe before any ask. Use *how* and *what* questions, not yes/no. *"It sounds like the renewal pressure is real and you need something to show before procurement closes."*
3. **During · state disagreements** — Name what you're actually disagreeing about; it's almost never the surface issue. *"I think we are disagreeing about whether field adoption or retention is the priority this quarter, not about the engineers themselves."*
4. **Stuck? · fall back on interests** — Acknowledge both have real constraints and neither wants to escalate. *"We both have legitimate constraints. What have we not considered yet?"*

## Sections 25–27 — LAB (Exercise 2) · 30 min · Negotiate an outcome

A **paired negotiation with asymmetric information** — each has a private briefing the other hasn't seen. **Agreement is not the goal; understanding each other well enough to name why is.**

1. **Read alone** — write your position, interest, BATNA, and hypothesis about your partner's interest
2. **Observe then ask** — lead with what you observe; use how/what questions before stating position
3. **Name the disagreement** — share interests, not positions
4. **Invite new options** — acknowledge shared constraints; invite options neither has proposed

**Debrief:** *At what point, if ever, did the conversation move from what you each needed to why you needed it?* → The conversation becomes an alignment when both parties focus on the problem they share rather than the outcome they want. **In both scenarios, the pairs were rarely in real conflict once the interests were on the table.**

---

## Section 28 — Key takeaways: Module 4 in four lines

1. **Alignment is won before the room, not in it.** Product leaders who map where power actually sits, formal and informal, earn a durable yes instead of a fragile one.
2. **Hard calls are not solved by more data; they are sharpened by weighing the cost your instinct skips.** Pricing the call across the org, relationships, and business is deciding with your eyes open.
3. **An executive narrative carries three things: the recommendation, the trade-off, and the reason to trust it.** Leading with the bottom line gets a decision, not just a hearing.
4. **The hardest part of a hard call is rarely the choosing; it is owning the outcome and carrying the weight.** Studying your calls afterward is how you grow from them instead of just surviving them.
