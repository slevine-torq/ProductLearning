## 1. Why this session matters

This is the module the entire certification hangs off. Every later session — roadmapping, team leadership, executive influence, financials, AI — is downstream of the strategy you build here. If the strategy is soft, everything built on top of it inherits the softness.

It's also the session where the job description quietly changes underneath you. The work that got you here — shipping features well, being the sharpest person on the solution — is *not* the work that makes you a leader. Four shifts define the transition:

- **Direction** — your job is no longer to do the work, it's to make the direction clear enough that others make good calls without you in the room.
- **Bets** — you stop shipping features and start owning the bets behind them: why this market, why this approach, why now.
- **Insight** — you stop reacting to whatever data lands on your desk and start deciding which questions are even worth asking.
- **Scope** — you stop owning one area and become responsible for how all the pieces cohere toward one direction.

By the end of the session you'll have written a one-page product strategy and started your final-project repo (`01-strategy/`). This pre-read is here so the frameworks — the hierarchy, the three tests, Playing to Win — already feel familiar when they hit the slide.

## 2. The one idea to walk in already chewing on

> **Strategy is a set of choices about what you will *not* do. If it says yes to everything, it hasn't made a choice.**

This is the reframe the whole module turns on, and it's harder to live than to nod along to. Most "strategies" are really wish lists in a nicer font: a vision, a stack of goals, and a roadmap where everything is a priority. The moment a strategy accommodates everyone, it has stopped being a strategy.

A real strategy has a *hard no* in it — one valuable thing you deliberately choose not to do, so the yes's have power. Notion chose not to compete on enterprise depth early. Figma chose not to build a desktop app. Those no's weren't limitations; they were the strategy's lever.

Sit with your own version before class: what is the valuable, defensible, tempting thing your product could do that you should say no to right now? If you can't name one, that's the tell that you have goals, not a strategy.

## 3. A short "noticing" exercise to do before class

Ten to fifteen minutes, no output required. Pick **two products you use every day** — one you love, one you tolerate — and try to reverse-engineer the strategy behind each. You're not writing anything; you're just training the muscle you'll use all session.

For each product, notice:

| Look for | The question underneath |
|---|---|
| **Who it's clearly for** | Can you name the specific user in a specific moment, or does it feel built for everyone? |
| **What it refuses to do** | Where has it obviously said no — a feature it won't add, a segment it ignores? |
| **What it's world-class at** | Name the one thing a competitor can't easily copy. If nothing comes to mind, that's interesting too. |
| **Where the money comes from** | Does the way it makes money reinforce the value, or fight it? |

The product you *tolerate* is usually the more instructive one — a tool that does everything for everyone and stands for nothing. When the "Playing to Win" cascade goes up in the room, you'll already have two live examples of what coherence and incoherence feel like.

## 4. Frameworks you'll keep hearing

You don't need to master these before class — that's what the session is for. But hearing them once now means the words won't trip you up in the room. Full definitions land in the `Glossary.md` afterward.

**The Hierarchy** — Vision → Strategy → Roadmap → Execution. Each layer only makes sense in the context of the one above it. The most common mistake is a roadmap with no strategy above it, or a strategy with no vision above that.

**Three tests for a defensible vision** — *Why now?* (what shifted in the last 2–3 years), *Why us?* (a genuine right to win), *Why us, now?* (is the org actually ready). If your vision can't pass all three, it isn't ready.

**Playing to Win** — The primary framework: five linked choices that must cohere top to bottom — winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities required, management systems. The moment one contradicts another, you've found a hole.

**Rumelt's kernel** — Lives inside the cascade: **diagnosis** (the real, uncomfortable challenge) → **guiding policy** (your approach) → **coherent actions** (mutually reinforcing moves).

**OKRs** — The bridge from strategy to execution. Objectives inspire; Key Results measure *outcomes* ("retention 34% → 62%"), not *outputs* ("ship X by Q2").

**Two-way / one-way door** — Reversible, cheap decisions vs. hard-to-undo ones. Match your rigor to the door.

## 5. Pick your scenario before you arrive

Your final project runs on one scenario, carried through all six modules. Come to Module 1 having already chosen, so you can spend the session building instead of deciding.

- **Fable (B2C) — The Growth Wall.** A mental-wellness app that grew fast, then worked *too* well: users resolved their acute need and quietly stopped opening it. The strategic question is retention and engagement — what the product becomes after the acute phase.
- **Meridian (B2B) — The Field Gap.** A construction project-management platform built for enterprise complexity that the people closest to the work — field superintendents and foremen — never adopted; they run their days on phone cameras and group texts. The strategic question is adoption and expansion.

You can also bring a real initiative from your own organization — same frameworks, same artifacts, your own metrics and context. If you go this route, pick something you actually have decision rights over, not a hypothetical.

## 6. Two questions to come ready to answer

These become warmups in the session, so an honest answer pays off in the room.

**Question 1:** *What did you see a product leader do that made you realize the job is fundamentally different from being a great PM?*

Be specific — a real moment, not a LinkedIn platitude. The best answers are usually about something a leader *didn't* do: didn't jump in with the solution, didn't chase the shiny request, didn't soften the diagnosis to keep the room comfortable.

**Question 2:** *For your chosen scenario, what's the one hard no?*

One deliberate trade-off, not a list of things you're deprioritizing. "We will not ___ because ___." You'll refine it in the session, but bring a first draft. The discomfort you feel writing it is the signal you're doing it right.
