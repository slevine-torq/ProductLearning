## Section 6 — Q&A: What it actually means to lead people · ACTIVITY · 10 min

Three discussion prompts:
- Think of the best manager you have had and describe one specific behavior that made them effective.
- When did you last give someone you *do not manage* feedback that was necessary but uncomfortable?
- What is one IC habit you had to actively force yourself to stop when you became a leader?

**Where the discussion lands:** Every situation in this module is one you already know is hard — giving feedback to someone you don't manage, watching a strong PM lose their edge, making a hiring call with incomplete information. Most leaders default to avoidance or improvisation, and both are expensive. Your value at this level is not whether you can handle these moments when they are easy. **It is whether you have a system for handling them when they are not.**

## Section 7 — Stop, Start, Continue · FRAMEWORK

The transition isn't learning new skills on top of existing ones. It requires putting down the habits that made you successful.

| **Stop** | **Start** | **Continue** |
|---|---|---|
| Solving the problem yourself | Creating context others can execute against | Being close enough to the work to know when someone needs help |
| Writing specs to show you understand | Building judgment in your PMs, not giving answers | Caring about the product outcome, not just the process |
| Being the loudest voice on the solution | Naming what good looks like before work begins | Keeping your own product thinking sharp |
| Measuring yourself by what you shipped | Measuring yourself by what your team produces | |
| Attending every product meeting | Protecting your team's time, including your own | |

**The hardest part is not learning what to start. It is genuinely putting down what you need to stop when everything in you still wants to pick it back up.**

## Section 8 — The player-coach dial · FRAMEWORK

The deliberate middle is not a fixed point. Four modes you move between **per person and per task**.

Failure modes at each end: **Over-Player** (still doing the IC work; bottleneck) ←→ **Over-Coach** (hands-off to the point of abdication).

| Mode | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | New person, new task | Clear direction gives them the context they need to move with confidence |
| **Coach** | Growth edge | Questions build their thinking more effectively than answers |
| **Support** | Complex ambiguity | Your presence and obstacle removal matters more than your judgment |
| **Delegate** | Strength and experience | Full ownership is both the most efficient and the most motivating thing you can give |

The same PM might need **Direct** on a new strategic problem and **Delegate** on their established domain. The skill is reading the situation and moving deliberately rather than defaulting to one mode.

## Section 9 — Self-ownership before action

Before you can show up well for anyone else, check your own thinking. Every time you act without it you introduce noise: confusion about expectations, quiet resentment, conversations that erode trust.

1. **Have I actually been clear?** If the expectation was never stated directly — in conversation or writing — the gap may be yours to close first.
2. **Have I let this go on too long?** If the early conversation was avoided, the other person has had no signal anything was wrong, and the pattern has had time to solidify.
3. **Is my frustration with their behavior or a decision I made?** If a resourcing, scope, or direction call landed on them in a way that *looks* like a performance issue, the source may be yours to own.
4. **Am I doing this to help them or to relieve my own discomfort?** Both can be true, but if it's mostly the second, the feedback will land as an attack rather than support.

## Section 11 — When things go wrong, name the type first · FRAMEWORK

The response that works for one type will make another type worse.

| Type | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| **Feedback problem** | The behavior keeps happening despite clear expectations. Your feedback hasn't been specific, timely, or grounded enough in observed behavior and real impact. **SBI is your tool here.** |
| **Clarity problem** | The gap exists because expectations, ownership, or decision rights were never made explicit. They are not failing to meet a standard — **they are operating without one.** |
| **System problem** | Expectations are clear and feedback lands, but something in the environment makes it impossible to perform. Look at what you could remove, change, or fix before addressing behavior. |
| **Hiring mismatch** | After clear feedback, explicit expectations, and removed obstacles, the gap persists. The honest diagnosis shifts to role fit. Naming it clearly and early is what allows fairness. |

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** most situations that feel like feedback problems are clarity or system problems in disguise.

## Section 12 — Common feedback pitfalls, and SBI

Feedback is **a perishable good** — most useful within 48 hours, least useful six months later arriving as part of a formal process. Two failure modes, both more common the more senior the leader.

| **1 · Withholding** | **2 · Blurting** |
|---|---|
| Feedback never arrives; the team member fills the silence with their own interpretation. By the time the conversation happens, it lands as a surprise. | Withholding reaches its limit and a minor incident carries months of unspoken frustration. Emotionally accurate but factually imprecise. |
| Trust erodes quietly · Standards stay unclear · Receiver has no context | Receiver doesn't recognize themselves · Conflict becomes about the conversation · Behavior never gets addressed |

**The fix — SBI:** Situation (specific, recent) → Behavior (observed, not interpreted) → Impact (on team, product, relationship) → Question (open, not a directive).

> *"In Tuesday's planning session (S), you agreed to a scope change without flagging it to the team (B), and engineering spent two days building against the wrong brief (I). Help me understand what happened (Q)?"*

## Section 13 — A clear process for any performance gap · FRAMEWORK

Follow it consistently and you will rarely need step five.

| # | Step | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Real-time SBI feedback** | Within 48 hours of the observation. *Most skipped.* |
| 2 | **State-of-the-relationship conversation** | Name the pattern directly in a dedicated conversation, not a roadmap check-in. *Most feared.* |
| 3 | **Documented shared understanding** | Write down what changes, by when, what support exists, what happens if the gap persists. Both parties confirm in writing. **A shared agreement, not a formal PIP.** *Most avoided.* |
| 4 | **N-day check-in** | Set the date in the conversation itself rather than waiting for the next review cycle. Makes the commitment real. *Most delayed.* |
| 5 | **Formal PIP** | Only if the gap persists after all four. By this point it has context and documentation, so it shouldn't surprise anyone. *Last resort.* |

## Section 14 — Supporting your team at their best

Every hard conversation exists in service of this: a team performing well, growing, and feeling genuinely seen.

- **Recognition that lands** — Name the specific *behavior*, not just the outcome, and do it publicly. Make it specific enough to prove you were actually paying attention.
- **Development that stretches** — Give strong performers scope and visibility *before they ask*. Make growth feel inevitable, not something to be negotiated.

> **The strongest person on your team is often the most at risk.** They quietly absorb the hardest problems while everything looks fine from the outside, and rarely tell you because the pressure to perform is already on them. Watch the signals instead: energy shifts, shorter responses, less initiative.

## Section 15 — LAB (Exercise 1) · 15 min · Diagnose a team situation and make a call

Apply a structured approach to a **real** leadership situation you're facing or recently went through.

1. **Describe the situation** — one paragraph: who's involved, what you observed, how long it's been happening. Facts, not interpretation.
2. **Diagnose it** — use the four-type framework; write one sentence explaining your reasoning.
3. **Write your next action** — one action for the next two weeks, plus the first sentence of the conversation you need to have.
4. **Rehearse with AI** — run the role-play prompt and note how the conversation lands over three exchanges.

*If you haven't yet led a team, use a situation you experienced as an IC and apply the framework from the leader's perspective.*

## Section 20 — Every team structure is a bet · FRAMEWORK

Speed, ownership, coordination, or scale. **The question isn't which model is right. It's whether your current structure was chosen deliberately, or just happened.**

| Structure | Optimizes for | Trade-off | Real example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome-Oriented** | Ownership + accountability | Duplication risk across squads | Spotify squad model |
| **Feature-Oriented** | Speed + clear scope | Cross-team coherence challenge | Amazon two-pizza teams |
| **Platform + Product** | Shared infrastructure + reduced dependencies | Slower platform-layer velocity | Airbnb platform team |
| **Small Outcome Team** | High trust + minimal layers + max speed | Coordination costs compound | Linear |

## Section 21 — Setting expectations when expanding your team

**Hire someone great for the job you actually have, not the job you imagine or aspire for it to be.** Five dimensions to clarify before the process starts — the clearer you are, the stronger the brief you hand a recruiter.

1. **Direction ambiguity** — Is the strategy clear enough to execute, or does this role need to help set it first? Hiring an executor into a strategy vacuum produces frustration on both sides.
2. **Growth stages** — 0→1 builder, 1→10 scaler, or 10→100 optimizer? Each requires a fundamentally different operating mode, and the wrong match is visible within ninety days.
3. **Market ambiguity** — Generate market insight from scratch, or apply insight the company already has? The first needs a researcher's instinct, the second a strategist's.
4. **Team ambiguity** — Be upfront about the team structure this person is stepping into. The type of team should directly shape the profile.
5. **Org ambiguity** — Be explicit about how influence *actually* works, not how it looks on the org chart. **The gap between the two is where new hires lose momentum fastest.**

*Source: Nikhil Singhal, Meta.*

## Section 22 — Your team charter · FRAMEWORK

A good charter works because it is **specific enough to be tested against reality** — not values to aspire to, but agreements governing how work actually gets done.

| Component | ✗ Too vague | ✓ Specific enough |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · What We Own** | "The core app experience" | "We own onboarding through Day 30. Out of scope: AI prompt engine (Data team), push strategy (CRM team). Cross-boundary decisions need a joint call, not unilateral action." |
| **2 · How We Decide** | "Collaboratively" | "PM decides scope within quarter alone. Cross-team conflicts escalate within 24h by a synchronous call, not a Slack thread." |
| **3 · What Success Looks Like** | "Increase engagement" | "Day-30 retention: 22% → 38%. Weekly sessions per retained user: 1.2 → 2.8. Miss all three: replan before Q4 starts." |
| **4 · How We Work** | "We value transparency" | "We raise problems when they are small. We disagree in the room, not in Slack after. We protect deep work from 10am to 1pm." |

**The test: could a new PM on day one read this and know exactly what to do?**

## Sections 23–25 — LAB (Exercise 2) · 20 min · Draft your team charter

Draft the **two hardest components** — "What We Own" and "How We Decide" — then refine until every line is specific enough to resolve a real decision or conflict.

1. **Review the tension** — read the cross-team tension for your scenario. Understanding the tension is what makes the charter useful.
2. **Generate a first draft** — feed in your M1 strategy and M2 roadmap.
3. **Refine until specific** — every line must resolve a real decision or conflict, not read as a generic role description.

**Show and swap (5 min):** cold-read a peer's charter silently for 1 minute, no verbal context. Then discuss: *where does the charter leave room for interpretation that could cause a conflict?*

## Section 26 — Key takeaways: Module 3 in four lines

1. **The habits that made you great as an IC are the exact habits that will limit you as a leader.** The transition is not adding new skills. It is putting down what you need to stop when everything in you wants to pick it back up.
2. **Feedback is the most direct lever a product leader has on team performance.** Those who do the internal work before the hard conversation are the ones whose feedback actually lands.
3. **Strong performers rarely signal when they are struggling because the pressure to perform is already on them.** Product leaders who watch the signals protect the people their teams depend on most.
4. **Structure and accountability systems are the hard skills that make soft skills sustainable.** Product leaders who codify ownership, decision rights, and success criteria into a team charter stop managing by instinct and start leading by design.
