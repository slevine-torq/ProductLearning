## 01 · The Behaviors That Define Product Leadership

### {06} What it actually means to lead people

A few questions worth sitting with: think of the best manager you have had and the one specific behavior that made them effective. When did you last give someone you do not manage feedback that was necessary but uncomfortable? What is one IC habit you had to actively force yourself to stop when you became a leader?

The situations in this module are ones every product leader already knows are hard: giving feedback to someone you do not manage, watching a strong PM lose their edge, making a hiring call with incomplete information. The difficulty is not a surprise. What is missing is a repeatable way through them. Most leaders default to avoidance or improvisation, and both are expensive. Avoidance lets problems compound quietly. Improvisation produces inconsistent outcomes that erode trust.

Your value at this level is not whether you can handle these situations when they are easy. It is whether you have a system for handling them when they are not. This module is about building that system.

### {07} Stop, Start, Continue

Making the transition from IC to product leader is not about learning new skills on top of the ones you have. It requires putting down the habits that made you successful in the first place.

**Stop.** Solving the problem yourself. Writing specs to show you understand. Being the loudest voice on the solution. Measuring yourself by what you shipped. Attending every product meeting. These behaviors were right for so long that putting them down feels like regression. But every time you write the spec yourself, the PM watching you learns you do not trust them to write it. Every time you solve the problem in the room, you remove the moment where someone else develops the judgment to solve it next time. The damage is invisible in the short term and compounding in the long term.

**Start.** Creating context others can execute against. Building judgment in your PMs, not giving answers. Naming what good looks like before work begins. Measuring yourself by what your team produces. Protecting your team's time, including your own. This is not the opposite of Stop. It is a fundamentally different way of measuring your own contribution. None of it feels like real work at first, because you cannot point to something you personally shipped. But one product leader who creates clear context and builds strong judgment in five PMs produces more than one who does the work of two PMs themselves.

**Continue.** Being close enough to the work to know when someone needs help. Caring about the product outcome, not just the process. Keeping your own product thinking sharp. The pendulum can swing too far: some leaders overcorrect and become so hands-off that they lose touch with the work, stop having opinions, and the team stops bringing them into real conversations. Staying sharp, close, and genuinely curious about the product outcome is what keeps you credible.

The hardest part of this transition is not learning what to start. It is genuinely putting down what you need to stop when everything in you still wants to pick it back up.

### {08} The player-coach dial

Every product leader sits somewhere on this dial, and most land closer to one end than the other based on how they learned to lead. Neither extreme is wrong by intention; they just produce different outcomes than what most leaders are trying to achieve.

On the over-player end, you are still doing a lot of the IC work yourself: writing the spec, taking the hard stakeholder call, solving the design problem. Your team may feel supported, but over time they under-develop, because the moments where they would build judgment keep getting resolved by you. On the over-coach end, you give your team a lot of space. That feels like trust, and often it is, but without enough presence and direction, problems compound quietly and your team can start to feel directionless without being able to name why.

The deliberate middle is not a fixed point. It is four modes you move between depending on who you are working with and what they are working on:

- **Direct:** when someone is new to a role or task, clear direction gives them the context to move with confidence.
- **Coach:** when someone is on their growth edge, questions build their thinking more effectively than answers.
- **Support:** when someone is navigating genuine complexity, your presence and obstacle removal matters more than your judgment.
- **Delegate:** when someone has proven strength and experience, full ownership is both the most efficient and the most motivating thing you can give them.

The same PM might need direct leadership on a new strategic problem and full delegation on their established domain. The skill is reading the situation and moving deliberately between modes rather than defaulting to one.

### {09} Self-ownership before action

The dial tells you how to show up for your team. But before you can show up well for anyone else, you have to check your own thinking first. Every time you act without it, you introduce noise: confusion about expectations, resentment that builds quietly, conversations that erode trust instead of building it. Four questions to run first:

- **Have I actually been clear?** Assumption is not communication. If the expectation was never stated directly, state it explicitly before opening any feedback conversation. You cannot hold someone accountable for a standard they were never given.
- **Have I let this go on too long?** The longer a pattern goes unaddressed, the more the other person has been operating without any signal that something is wrong. If the answer is yes, acknowledge it in the conversation. It opens the door rather than putting them on the defensive.
- **Is my frustration with their behavior or with a decision I made?** If a resourcing call, unclear scope, or shifting direction created the problem, own that first.
- **Am I doing this to help them or to relieve my own discomfort?** If the honest answer is mostly the second, wait. Come back when you can name specifically what you want to change and why it matters for them, not just for you.

The leaders whose teams trust them most are the ones who do this work consistently, not just when a conversation feels high stakes.

## 02 · Leading People Through Performance

### {11} When things go wrong, name the type first

Once you know you are showing up from the right place, identify what you are actually dealing with before you act, because the response that works for one type of situation will make another type worse.

- **Feedback problem.** The behavior keeps happening despite clear expectations. The issue is not the person's awareness of the standard; it is that your feedback has not been specific, timely, or grounded enough in observed behavior and real impact to change what they do.
- **Clarity problem.** The gap exists because expectations, ownership, or decision rights were never made explicit. They are not failing to meet a standard. They are operating without one.
- **System problem.** Your expectations are clear and your feedback is landing, but something in the environment makes it impossible to perform. Look at what you could remove, change, or fix before addressing the behavior.
- **Hiring mismatch.** After clear feedback, explicit expectations, and removed obstacles, the gap still persists. The honest diagnosis shifts to role fit. Naming it clearly and early is what allows fairness rather than letting it drag on for both of you.

Watch out: most situations that feel like feedback problems are clarity or system problems in disguise. Getting the diagnosis right is what makes everything that follows land.

### {12} Common feedback pitfalls, and the SBI fix

The single most direct lever a product leader has on team performance is feedback. Not strategy, not process, not headcount. Feedback is a perishable good, most useful within 48 hours of the observation and least useful six months later when it arrives as part of a formal process. Two failure modes show up consistently, and both are more common the more senior the leader.

**Withholding** feels responsible: you are waiting for the right moment and enough evidence. But the team member is not waiting with you. They are filling the silence with their own interpretation, and it is almost never accurate. By the time the conversation finally happens, it lands as a verdict on a pattern they had no idea existed.

**Blurting** is the release valve for withholding, where a minor incident becomes the moment everything comes out at once. The frustration is real and the pattern is usually valid, but the delivery is so disconnected from the specific incident that the receiver cannot find themselves in it. They defend the incident, and the real conversation never happens.

The fix is **SBI**, because it anchors the conversation to observable reality rather than interpretation:

- **Situation:** anchor to something specific and recent.
- **Behavior:** describe what was observed, not what was interpreted.
- **Impact:** connect the behavior to the team, the product, or the working relationship.
- **Question:** end with an open question rather than a directive.

In practice: "In Tuesday's planning session, you agreed to a scope change without flagging it to the team, and engineering spent two days building against the wrong brief. Help me understand what happened." The situation is specific, the behavior observable, the impact on the work, and the question opens the conversation rather than closing it. Leading high-performing teams is not about avoiding hard conversations. It is about having them well, early, and consistently enough that they stop feeling hard.

### {13} A clear process for any performance gap

Every team faces a performance gap at some point. The question is not whether it will happen but whether you have a clear, fair, consistent way to address it. This sequence supports the person, sets clear expectations, and produces better outcomes.

1. **Real-time SBI feedback.** Give specific, behavior-based feedback within 48 hours. The most skipped step, because it requires acting while the situation is still uncomfortable. The longer you wait, the harder it is for the other person to connect the feedback to their experience.
2. **State-of-the-relationship conversation.** A dedicated conversation that names the pattern directly, not a difficult topic bolted onto a roadmap check-in. The most feared step, and often the one that most turns things around, because it signals you are invested enough to address it directly.
3. **Documented shared understanding.** Write down what changes, by when, what support exists, and what happens if the gap persists, and have both parties confirm it. The most avoided step, but the clearest act of respect: here is exactly what we agreed, and here is how I will support you.
4. **N-day check-in.** Set the date before you leave the room. A commitment without a deadline gets interpreted differently by both parties over the following weeks. The check-in makes it concrete.
5. **Formal PIP.** If the gap persists after all four steps, a formal PIP is the right next move, and by this point it has full context and will not come as a surprise.

Follow this consistently and you will rarely need step five. More importantly, you will have given the person every real opportunity to succeed, which is the point of performance management in the first place.

### {14} Supporting your team at their best

Every hard conversation in this section exists in service of this moment: a team that is performing well, growing, and feels genuinely seen.

**Recognition that lands.** Most leaders believe they recognize their people well; most teams experience that recognition as generic and quickly forgotten. The difference is specificity. When you name the exact behavior in the exact moment, publicly, you tell that person you were paying close enough attention to notice. That changes the relationship, not just the moment.

**Development that stretches.** Strong performers who feel like they are growing stay. Strong performers who feel like they are delivering but standing still start looking elsewhere, usually quietly and before you see it coming. Do not wait to be asked; create the conditions for growth before the conversation about it needs to happen.

**Protect your strongest performers.** The strongest person on your team is often the most at risk. They quietly absorb the hardest problems while everything looks fine from the outside, and rarely tell you because the pressure to perform is already on them. By the time they say something is wrong, it has usually been building for months. Watch the signals instead: energy shifts, shorter responses, less initiative.

### {15} Exercise · Diagnose a team situation and make a call

Apply a structured approach to a real leadership situation you are currently facing or have recently been through. Your goal is to name the situation clearly, diagnose it honestly, and leave having tested the first sentence of the conversation you need to have against how the other person is likely to respond.

1. Write one paragraph describing the situation: who is involved, what you have observed, and how long it has been happening. Stick to facts, not interpretation.
2. Diagnose it using the four-type framework, and write one sentence explaining your reasoning. The most common mistake is jumping straight to "feedback problem" when the real issue is clarity or system. Sit with all four first.
3. Write one next action for the next two weeks, and the first sentence of the conversation you need to have. The first sentence is the hardest and most important part, since it sets the tone for everything that follows.
4. Run the AI role-play prompt from your guide and note how the conversation lands over three exchanges.

If you have not yet led a team, use a situation you experienced as an IC and apply the framework from the leader's perspective.

## 03 · Creating the Conditions for Great Work

### {20} What are you optimizing for? Team structures

As a product leader, you will most likely inherit your team structure rather than choose it. You still have the opportunity to examine it and make a deliberate choice, even if it lands on the same model. To make that choice, ask what you are actually optimizing for: speed, ownership, coordination, or scale.

- **Outcome-Oriented** optimises for ownership and accountability, because PMs are accountable for a result, not a feature set. Trade-off: two squads on adjacent outcomes can solve the same problem in different ways. The antidote is strong cross-squad communication rituals, not a restructure. *Spotify's squad model made the connection between the team and the outcome they own visible.*
- **Feature-Oriented** optimises for speed and clear scope, because it eliminates ambiguity about ownership at the moment a decision needs to be made. Trade-off: cross-team coherence. *Amazon's two-pizza rule works because the constraint forces every decision about what to build to become a decision about what not to build.*
- **Platform + Product** optimises for shared infrastructure and reduced dependencies, stopping every product team from rebuilding the same foundation independently. Trade-off: slower platform-layer velocity. *Airbnb restructured this way when growth and core product teams kept rebuilding the same search and payments infrastructure.*
- **Small Outcome Team** optimises for high trust, minimal layers, and maximum speed, because decisions happen in hallway conversations instead of planning meetings. Trade-off: coordination costs compound as you grow. *Linear hires slowly, stays small deliberately, and treats the absence of process as a feature.*

Every team structure is a bet on what matters most. The question isn't which model is right. It's whether your current structure was chosen deliberately, or just happened, because that determines whether the conditions for great work exist by design or by accident.

### {21} How to set expectations when expanding your team

One of the most direct ways to build the conditions for great work is to be intentional about who you bring in and what you set them up to succeed at. Hire someone great for the job you actually have, not the job you imagine or aspire for it to be. The clearer you are on these five dimensions before the process starts, the stronger the brief and the more likely the right profile surfaces.

- **Direction ambiguity:** clarify whether the strategy is clear enough to execute or whether this role requires someone to help set it first. Hiring an executor into a strategy vacuum produces frustration on both sides.
- **Growth stages:** decide whether you need a 0 to 1 builder, a 1 to 10 scaler, or a 10 to 100 optimizer. Each stage requires a different operating mode, and the wrong match is visible within ninety days.
- **Market ambiguity:** determine whether you need someone to generate market insight from scratch or apply insight the company already has. The first requires a researcher's instinct, the second a strategist's.
- **Team ambiguity:** be upfront about the team structure this person is stepping into and what their role within it requires. The type of team should shape the profile you hire for.
- **Org ambiguity:** be explicit about how influence actually works in your organization, not how it looks on the org chart. The gap between the two is where new hires lose momentum fastest.

Score a role you recently hired for against these five dimensions, then ask whether your interview questions actually tested for what you defined. When these are not resolved before the process starts, interviewers default to assessing for someone they would enjoy working with. That is not culture fit; it is cognitive bias, and one of the most expensive hiring mistakes a product leader can make. *(Source: Nikhil Singhal, Meta.)*

### {22} Your team charter: what good looks like

Leading well requires both soft and hard skills. The feedback conversations, the development investments, and the psychological safety you build create the conditions; but conditions without structure are fragile. A team charter is the hard skill that makes those conditions durable. A good charter works because it is specific enough to be tested against reality.

**What We Own.** Ownership ambiguity is the most common source of cross-team friction. "The core app experience" is not a scope definition. Compare: "We own onboarding through Day 30. Out of scope: AI prompt engine (Data team), push strategy (CRM team). Cross-boundary decisions need a joint call, not unilateral action." The moment you name the specific surface, the out-of-scope areas, and the escalation path, your team stops having to ask you what they own.

**How We Decide.** Without explicit decision rights, your team relitigates decisions that should have been made once. "Collaboratively" tells no one what happens when two people disagree at 4pm on a Friday. Compare: "PM decides scope within the quarter alone. Cross-team conflicts escalate within 24h by a synchronous call, not a Slack thread."

**What Success Looks Like.** When success is vague, teams optimize for activity rather than outcome. "Increase engagement" gives no way to know when you have succeeded. Compare: "Day-30 retention: 22% to 38%. Weekly sessions per retained user: 1.2 to 2.8. Miss all three: replan before Q4 starts."

**How We Work.** Behavioral commitments only mean something when they are specific enough to evaluate your own behavior against on any given day. "We value transparency" means something different to everyone. Compare: "We raise problems when they are small. We disagree in the room, not in Slack after. We protect deep work from 10am to 1pm."

The test: could a new PM on day one read this and know exactly what to do? Start with "What We Own," because everything else depends on getting that one right.

## 04 · Hands-On Lab: Draft Your Team Charter

### {24} Draft your team charter

Use AI to generate a first draft of the two most critical components of your team charter for your chosen scenario, then refine it until it feels specific enough to be real. Your goal is charter language specific enough that a new PM on day one could read it and know exactly what they own and how decisions get made. You'll need your M1 strategy and M2 roadmap.

1. Review the cross-team tension for your scenario in the lab guide. Understanding the tension is what makes the charter useful, not just filling in the table.
2. Use the prompt in your lab guide to generate a first draft of your "What We Own" and "How We Decide" components. Feed in your M1 strategy, M2 roadmap, and the cross-team tension so the charter reflects your specific direction.
3. Refine the output until every line is specific enough to resolve a real decision or conflict. Save your completed charter; it becomes part of your project deliverables.

### {25} Show and swap your team charter

To validate whether your charter is specific enough to stand on its own without context, pair up with a peer and let them cold-read it. Swap your components, then read your partner's silently for one minute with no verbal context or explanations, before you discuss. Then swap.

The question to answer: where does the charter leave room for interpretation that could cause a conflict? If your partner can identify an ambiguity in the ownership or decision rights that would cause a real problem in week one, that is exactly the signal you need to tighten it.

### {26} Key takeaways

- **The habits that made you great as an IC are the exact habits that will limit you as a leader.** The transition is not adding new skills. It is putting down what you need to stop when everything in you wants to pick it back up.
- **Feedback is the most direct lever a product leader has on team performance.** Those who do the internal work before the hard conversation are the ones whose feedback actually lands.
- **Strong performers rarely signal when they are struggling** because the pressure to perform is already on them. Product leaders who watch the signals protect the people their teams depend on most.
- **Structure and accountability systems are the hard skills that make soft skills sustainable.** Product leaders who codify ownership, decision rights, and success criteria into a team charter stop managing by instinct and start leading by design.

### {27} Keep practicing

Two optional exercises to go deeper:

1. **Complete your full team charter.** Take the two components you drafted and add the remaining two: what success looks like and how we work. Write specific, testable language, then check the finished charter against the same question: where does it leave room for interpretation that could cause a conflict?
2. **Run the self-check before your next hard conversation.** Before your next difficult conversation with someone on or adjacent to your team, work through the self-ownership questions. Write down your honest answers first, and compare them to how the conversation actually went afterwards.
