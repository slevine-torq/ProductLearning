## 01 · What is Product Strategy?

### Slide 08 — The leap to leadership

What did you see a product leader doing that made you realize the job was fundamentally different from being a great PM?

What makes the leadership role fundamentally different is not seniority or scope. It is a complete change in what the job is for. Product leaders are no longer responsible for the quality of their own output. They are responsible for the quality of the direction others execute against. When that direction is clear, teams move fast and make good decisions without needing the leader in every room. When it is not, the best execution in the world produces the wrong thing.

The value at this level is not how well you do the work. It is how well you set the conditions for others to do theirs. That starts with strategy.

### Slide 09 — What actually changes when you lead

When it comes time to craft your strategy, four shifts define how you operate.

**Direction.** Strategy is about setting a direction others can execute against without you in every room. Your job is no longer to do the work. It is to make the direction so clear that others can make good decisions from it independently.

**Bets.** Strategy is about making deliberate choices on where to play and how to win. You stop shipping features and start owning the bets behind them: why this market, why this approach, why now.

**Insight.** Strategy is about deciding what questions are worth asking in the first place. You stop reacting to data that lands in front of you and start defining what the team should be trying to learn and why it matters.

**Scope.** Strategy is about holding coherence across multiple teams and workstreams. You stop owning one area and start being responsible for how all the pieces fit together toward a single direction.

These four shifts are what separate a product leader who has a strategy from one who just has a roadmap.

### Slide 10 — The product strategy hierarchy

Before diving into frameworks, it helps to understand what product strategy actually is and how it connects to everything else your team does.

**Vision** is aspirational and stable. It defines who you serve, what problem you solve, and why now. It should not change every quarter.

**Strategy** sits below vision. It is the set of informed principles that guide how you deliver value, how you differentiate, and how you make money. It is adaptable as you learn, but it is not a roadmap.

**Roadmap** is where strategy becomes specific: products, features, milestones, timelines, owners. It empowers the team to execute against the strategy.

**Execution** is where the work happens. What you learn in execution feeds back up into the roadmap and strategy, but it should never be driving the vision.

The most common mistake product leaders make is building a roadmap without a strategy above it, or a strategy without a vision above that. Each layer only makes sense in the context of the one above it.

### Slide 11 — Three tests for a defensible vision

Your vision is not your mission statement. It is a bet on the future that justifies the strategy you are about to make. A simple way to keep them straight: vision is visual. You should be able to close your eyes and picture the world your product is trying to create. If you can't, it's probably a mission statement. To make sure that bet is a good one, run it through three tests before you commit.

**Why now? (Market timing.)** A vision tethered to a shift that already happened, or hasn't arrived yet, is not a vision, it is a description. Zoom had the right idea in 2013, but the world caught up to them in 2020. The bet only became undeniable when remote work became the default overnight. Ask what has shifted in the last two to three years that makes this the right moment.

**Why us? (Competitive position.)** Do you have a genuine right to win in this space? Vision is not aspiration detached from reality. Figma did not set out to build a better design tool. It bet that design could become a team sport, and that bet only made sense coming from a browser-native product. Ask what you understand about this market that others have not seen yet.

**Why us, now? (Org readiness.)** Can your team actually begin executing on this vision now? Ambition without readiness is a fantasy. A 12-person team declaring they will be the global standard has ambition but no anchor. The same team committing to own one city by end of year has a vision they can move toward. Ask what would need to be true inside the org to make this achievable.

If your vision cannot pass all three tests, it is not ready.

### Slide 12 — The process you run to craft strategy

Now you know what strategy is and what it is not. Here is how you actually build one. We'll trace each step through Notion's early strategy.

**Set your product vision.** This is your starting point and your filter. Every decision in the next five steps should trace back to it. If a bet cannot be justified by the vision, it does not belong in the strategy. Notion's vision was a single connected workspace where everything is a block and every block is composable, simple enough to settle any disagreement about what to build next.

**Generate insights.** You are looking for a point of view only your team can hold, one that synthesizes what you know about the market, the competition, and your own capabilities into something actionable. Notion's insight: wikis, docs, and databases were artificially separate, and the switching cost between them was where productivity died.

**Identify challenges.** This is where most strategies get soft. Naming the real obstacles requires honesty about what is actually hard. Notion's honest diagnosis was that a blocks-first architecture meant walking away from legacy formats entirely, which made early adoption harder. Naming that clearly is what let them design around it.

**Define approaches.** This is your where to play and how to win. Be explicit about what you are choosing not to do, because the no's are what give the yes's their power. Notion's bet was opinionated simplicity over feature parity: they chose not to compete on enterprise depth early, which kept the product coherent while competitors chased Confluence feature for feature.

**Build accountability.** Strategy that does not cascade into OKRs with clear owners and review cadences will not survive the quarter. Kill criteria matter here too. Notion cascaded their bet into a sequence: individuals first, viral spread through templates, then teams, then companies, with enterprise only after viral proof.

**Share early, iterate.** Get it in front of people before it feels ready. The pushback is not a threat to the strategy. It is the first real test of whether it holds. Notion's template ecosystem was a feedback loop that told them which use cases were resonating and where to go next.

Every one of these six steps can now be accelerated with AI: from synthesizing insights, to pressure-testing your diagnosis, to cascading OKRs.

### Slide 13 — AI as your strategic co-pilot

The best product leaders have always been the ones who can synthesize the most information into the clearest direction. AI does not change that. It removes the ceiling on how much ground you can cover.

**Synthesizing information.** No product leader can read every market report, customer interview, and internal data pull that crosses their desk. AI can. Use it to continuously surface what matters most across your market, customers, and org. Your job is to make the call. AI makes sure you are making it with the fullest possible picture.

**Stress-testing your thinking.** Before you defend your strategy in a room, use AI to attack it. Ask it to argue the case your CFO would make, or the objection your biggest competitor would raise, or to find the assumption most likely to be wrong. The goal is to find the soft spots before someone else does.

**Competitive monitoring.** Most teams do competitive analysis inconsistently. AI turns it into a continuous feed. Point it at competitor newsletters, social profiles, and product updates and you stop being surprised by moves you should have seen coming.

Used well, AI does not replace your strategic judgment. It expands the surface area you can think across and sharpens the quality of the bets you make.

## 02 · Playing to Win

### Slide 15 — The Playing to Win cascade

When it comes to crafting a product strategy, there is no shortage of frameworks. Playing to Win stands out because it does not just help you think through your strategy, it forces you to make choices and make them visible, through five questions that cascade and cohere from top to bottom. The moment one contradicts another, you know your strategy has a hole. We'll make each choice concrete with a fictitious app, HealthTrack.

**Winning Aspiration** defines what winning looks like in the customer's terms, not the internal metrics you want to see. HealthTrack's: being the app recreational athletes open on race day because it tells them the truth, not because their trainer told them to. That is a customer-felt definition of winning, not a revenue target.

**Where to Play** gets specific about which segment, geography, channel, and use case you serve, and which you will not. For HealthTrack: recreational runners and cyclists, aged 28 to 45, US and UK only, mobile-first, focused on race-day and training-week workflows. They explicitly ruled out elite athletes and gym-based fitness. Those boundaries are what make the strategy credible.

**How to Win** names your differentiator: the durable advantage competitors cannot easily replicate. Everything below this point serves it. HealthTrack's answer is the integration of heart-rate, pace, and sleep in one training signal, built on six years of data competitors cannot buy or build overnight. That is not a feature. It is a structural advantage.

**Capabilities Required** names what you must be world-class at to execute your How to Win, not what sounds impressive. For HealthTrack: predictive readiness modeling, partnerships with Garmin and Whoop for data access, and a race-specific content team. If a capability does not connect back to the How to Win, it does not belong.

**Management Systems** define the metrics, rituals, and structures that keep your choices alive quarter to quarter. HealthTrack runs a weekly retention review by cohort, a monthly prediction-accuracy score, and a quarterly win/loss post-mortem.

This framework was developed by Roger Martin and A.G. Lafley in their book *Playing to Win*, a recommended read if you want to go deeper.

### Slide 16 — Two more lenses for your toolkit

Playing to Win is not the only lens available. Depending on the strategic question you are facing, these two may be a better fit.

**Jobs-to-be-Done.** You may have personas, surveys, and usage data and still not know what the customer is actually hiring your product to do. Not what features they use, but what progress they are trying to make, functionally, socially, and emotionally. When a team cannot agree on what to build next, it is usually because nobody has answered that clearly enough. The obvious answer to "what does Spotify do" is stream music, but users were hiring it for the right song for every moment of their day: a workout, a commute, a dinner party. That insight shaped playlists, discovery, and recommendations. *Use it when your team debates features or personas but cannot agree on what the customer actually needs.*

**Platform vs. Product thinking.** A product is built to solve one problem well. A platform is built so others can solve problems on top of it. The choice shapes your roadmap, data strategy, and competitive position for years. Salesforce launched as a CRM built to solve one problem; opening it as a platform with AppExchange turned it into an ecosystem competitors could not replicate. *Use it when facing a decision about how broadly to scope your architecture, data, or ecosystem.*

### Slide 17 — Strategy autopsy: Figma vs. Adobe XD

This is one of the cleanest strategy autopsies in recent product history, because both companies operated in the same market, at the same time, with access to the same information. The gap in outcomes comes down entirely to the quality of their strategy.

Figma's diagnosis was uncomfortable to make in 2016: that the way design had always worked, file-based, tool-siloed, passed around in Slack and email, was the actual problem. A fundamental workflow problem, not a feature problem. Embedded in it was a bolder bet: design should not be the exclusive domain of designers. PMs, engineers, and stakeholders should sit inside the same file and contribute without a design background or a paid seat. Adobe's entire business model assumed creative tools were for creatives. Figma bet against that entirely.

From the diagnosis, the guiding policy followed directly: make design a team sport, browser-first and multiplayer by default. Not a better Sketch. A completely different model. And because the policy was that specific, the coherent actions wrote themselves: launch in the browser with no install friction, put prototyping and dev handoff in the same file, price per editor rather than per seat so adoption incentives aligned. The constraint of no desktop app was not a limitation. It was the strategy's lever.

Adobe was not incompetent. They had Creative Cloud, and with it the designer's trust, billing relationship, and daily workflow. But their diagnosis was "Figma is growing and Sketch users are switching," which is a market observation, not a diagnosis. Adobe never challenged its own core assumption that design tools were for designers, the very thing Figma was dismantling. Because the diagnosis was shallow, the guiding policy had no conviction, and the coherent actions never materialized: co-editing, cloud storage, and auto-animate, each a direct response to a Figma feature, each feeling like catch-up. The $20 billion acquisition attempt in 2022 was the clearest admission that the build strategy had failed. When regulators blocked it in 2023, XD was discontinued shortly after. Resources without a guiding policy is motion, not progress.

Rumelt's kernel is the lens that explains both: your **diagnosis** sharpens Where to Play, your **guiding policy** is How to Win, and your **coherent actions** are the capabilities and systems that make it real.

### Slide 20 — Choose your scenario

Your final project runs on one scenario, carried through all six modules. Pick the one that best fits your interests or current work.

**Scenario 1 · Fable (B2C): The Growth Wall.** Fable is a mental wellness app that grew fast during a period of heightened anxiety. Then its core value prop worked too well: users resolved their acute need and quietly stopped opening the app. Define what the product becomes for users after the acute phase, without losing the simplicity and trust that got them there. *Strategic focus: retention + engagement.*

**Scenario 2 · Meridian (B2B): The Field Gap.** Meridian is a construction project management platform built for enterprise complexity. But the people closest to the actual work, field superintendents and foremen on job sites, never adopted it; they run their days on phone cameras and group texts. Make the platform indispensable to field teams without stripping the enterprise depth your largest customers pay for. *Strategic focus: adoption + expansion.*

You can also bring a real initiative from your own organization. Same framework, same artifacts. You'll just bring your own metrics and context to each stage.

### Slide 21 — Exercise · Run your first strategic sprint

Write a one-page product strategy for either Fable Growth or Meridian Foundations. Your goal is a strategy specific enough that a skeptical board member could not poke a hole in it.

1. Choose your case: Fable Growth or Meridian Foundations. Commit to one.
2. Complete your one-page strategy using the five Playing to Win sections in your lab brief. Short, specific answers, not paragraphs.
3. Decide on your One Hard No: one deliberate trade-off, not a list of deprioritized items.

## 03 · From Strategy to Execution

### Slide 23 — OKRs bridge strategy and execution

A brilliant strategy that never cascades into what your teams build every day is just a document. OKRs are the management system that brings it to life.

Earlier we looked at strategy as a vertical hierarchy: vision at the top, execution at the bottom. Now look at it horizontally, because that is how it moves in practice. Strategy hands off to OKRs, OKRs to the roadmap, the roadmap to execution, each flowing into the next. Your job is to keep that flow intact and moving in one direction.

OKRs are the critical link because they translate strategic intent into measurable progress. Your strategy tells you where you are playing and how you plan to win. Your OKRs tell your teams what success looks like and how you will know when you have achieved it.

A key result is a measurable outcome, not a delivery milestone. "Ship readiness model by Q2" measures activity: you can hit it and still fail the user. "Race-week retention rises from 34% to 62%" measures a real change in behavior, the outcome the strategy promised.

**HealthTrack in practice.** The How to Win is specific: the only platform with integrated heart-rate, pace, and sleep, built for the race-week decision, winning on predictive readiness. Every OKR flows from that. Objective: become the app serious recreational athletes trust to make their race-week decisions. KR1: 7-day retention in the race-week cohort, 34% to 62%. KR2: readiness-model opens per user in race week, 1.2 to 4.5. KR3: NPS for race-week users, 31 to 58. Each measures whether the strategy is actually working.

### Slide 24 — How to make decisions without data

Product leaders are paid to decide, not to wait for certainty that never comes. Here is a repeatable framework for making defensible calls under ambiguity.

1. **Name what you know.** Separate fact from assumption. Write down only what is verifiably true, and label everything else as an assumption. This surfaces disagreements early, because two people arguing about a decision are often arguing about different assumptions they each believe are facts.
2. **Name what you don't know.** Map the unknowns into two buckets: knowable with research, and unknowns you have to live with. The knowable ones are worth a timebox before you decide. The structural ones you cannot resolve by waiting.
3. **Check reversibility.** Two-way-door decisions (reversible, low cost to undo) should be made fast and iterated on. One-way-door decisions (hard or expensive to reverse) warrant more rigor and time.
4. **Make a provisional bet and set a trigger.** Commit to your best call given what you know now. Then name explicitly what would cause you to change it: a metric that moves, a customer signal, a date to reassess. You are deciding in advance what being wrong looks like, so you can act on it quickly.

In your hands-on lab, the strategy you build is a provisional bet and the OKRs you write are the trigger. If your KRs are not moving by a set date, you revisit the strategy. That is the discipline.

### Slide 25 — Your strategy self-diagnostic in 6 questions

Before you declare any strategy done, run it through these six questions. This is everything above compressed into a checklist you can use on any strategy, at any point in your career.

- **Is it clear?** Can a new PM read it and know exactly what you will and won't do? If it needs a conversation to explain, it is not clear enough.
- **Does it make a hard bet?** Does it say no to something valuable? If it accommodates everything, it has not made a choice.
- **Does it name the real challenge?** Is the diagnosis specific enough to be uncomfortable? If anyone in your industry could have written it, it has not named the real problem.
- **Do the actions cohere?** Do all initiatives reinforce each other, or can you remove one without affecting the rest? A strategy is a system, not a list.
- **Can it be cascaded?** Can your team translate it directly into their own OKRs? If they struggle, it is not specific enough to drive execution.
- **Is it being committed to?** Are resources actually moving toward it? Resources tell the truth. Strategy without resources is theater.

## 04 · Hands-On Lab: Stress-Test Your Execution Plan

### Slide 27 — Exercise · Stress-test your execution plan

Return to your Playing to Win one-pager. Write three OKRs that flow directly from your strategy, then pressure-test the full picture using AI as your devil's advocate. Your goal is an OKR cascade tight enough that the AI cannot find a gap between your strategy and your execution plan.

1. Write three OKRs that flow directly from your strategy. Focus on outcomes, not outputs.
2. Run the AI pressure-test prompt from your lab brief in any LLM, and paste in your completed strategy.
3. Review the AI's challenges and decide what you would change and what you would defend.

The prompt is optimized for this exercise, but you're free to adjust it. The most useful reflection: which challenge is most valid, and does it change your bet or sharpen your defense of it?

### Slide 26 — Key takeaways

- **Product strategy is not a roadmap or a vision statement.** The most effective product leaders treat it as a deliberate set of choices about where to play, how to win, and what to say no to.
- **Good strategy starts with an honest diagnosis of the real challenge, not a description of market conditions.** Leaders who soften the diagnosis to protect feelings design a strategy that solves the wrong problem.
- **Playing to Win gives you a cascade of five choices that have to cohere from top to bottom.** Answer all five with specificity, show how they connect, and you have a strategy worth committing to.
- **OKRs are the bridge between strategic intent and the work teams ship every day.** Build that cascade clearly and your teams know exactly what they are optimizing for and why it matters.
