## 01 · From AI User to AI Leader

### {06} How AI shows up around you

Where has AI already changed how you work, and how widely is it used across the teams and functions around you?

Everyone reading this is already an AI user. You reach for it to draft, to research, to pressure-test your own thinking, and it has almost certainly made you faster at your own work. That is real, and for most people it is where the relationship with AI stops. Whether you lead a team today or are about to, the leverage moves from how well you use AI to what you make possible for the people around you. That is the shift this module works through: from AI as something you use to AI as something you lead.

### {07} The stages of AI leverage as a leader

As a PM you spend some of your time using AI to sharpen your own work, and it has made you faster at nearly every part of it. As a leader there is a new question worth sitting with: what happens to that skill when you are no longer the one doing the work?

- **Stage 1 · Personal Productivity.** This is the one you already know, because it is where your relationship with AI started. You and a rough idea become a clear PRD draft in minutes, close enough to react to and sharpen, work that used to cost the better part of an afternoon. That value is real and does not go away. It stops being where your impact lives the moment you are no longer the one doing the work.
- **Stage 2 · Team Capability.** Here the same capability stops being something you do and becomes something you set for a team. Every PM runs to the same standard, and each knows which calls they can trust to the model and which still need a human before anyone acts. You become the person who set how the whole team analyzes, which lifts the floor for everyone instead of raising only your own ceiling.
- **Stage 3 · Org Advantage.** This is the altitude that shows up in board conversations, where AI stops being a way your team works and becomes a bet the organization makes with money and roadmap attached. The advantage compounds rather than resetting every quarter.

Your leadership lives in Stages 2 and 3. The trap is running them on Stage 1 instincts: a leader who stays the best AI user on the team, rather than building the conditions for the team to use it well, quietly caps what the team can do at whatever they personally have time to model.

### {08} Where AI earns its place

Every one of these workflows is one you have already run for yourself, so you know what good looks like in each, which means you can tell in seconds whether AI's first pass holds up. That judgment is the asset, and it is the one thing your team cannot borrow from you. Stage 2 is turning it into something they can work from: you set how each workflow gets run and where a human still checks it.

- **Strategy:** the quality of a team's strategic thinking usually depends on which PM ran the analysis. When the standard for pressure-testing a landscape is the same for everyone, the team's judgment holds together even on the work you never see.
- **Prioritization & Roadmapping:** a hard cut gets reopened the moment someone senior pushes on it, unless the reasoning was built to be defended by whoever holds the line that day. Set that structure once and your PMs carry the decision through rooms you are not in.
- **Development & Coaching:** the one that stays closest to your own judgment. Coaching a specific person through a hard conversation is not something you standardize and hand off. What you set here is your own preparation, turning scattered signals into themes so you arrive clearer. Naming that honestly matters, because treating every workflow as a team standard is how leaders end up automating the judgment that most needs to stay human.
- **Stakeholder Alignment:** an uneven pre-read from one PM costs the whole team credibility others spent months earning. When the pre-read and objection set are built the same way every time, every request arrives already shaped for the person deciding it.
- **Financial Bets:** the gap in a funding conversation is between a PM who can assemble a business case and one who can hold it up when finance questions the assumption the whole bet rests on. Set the standard for how a case gets built and stress-tested, so the load-bearing assumption is named before someone else finds it.

Set one of these well and it keeps working long after you have moved on, because a standard the team holds to does not need your attention to stay alive.

### {09} Leading a team that builds with AI

Your leverage as a leader sits in the conditions you set for a team. When that team builds with AI, three are worth setting deliberately. Set them well and you can step back, because a team that knows the rules does not need you reviewing its work to stay safe. Leave them unset and the team fills the gaps with its own guesses, and you get pulled back into the detail.

- **Norms.** Say where AI output can ship on its own and where a human signs off first. Writing it down once replaces a hundred small approvals with a rule the team applies for itself. *When Chevrolet's dealership bot was talked into "selling" a car for a dollar, the failure was not the jailbreak; no one had drawn the line between what the AI could say and what it could commit to on the company's behalf.*
- **Risks.** The quiet failures are the dangerous ones: over-trust in a confident wrong answer, skills eroding as easy reps get automated, ownership blurring. A confident wrong answer is harder to catch than an obvious one, and a team that has stopped doing its own reps loses the ability to catch it. *Researchers documented an Aalto accounting team whose AI prompt produced good output for months, until the underlying model changed and quality quietly slipped. The team no longer caught it, because the skill to spot the miss had eroded while everything still looked fine.*
- **Culture.** Rules only cover the situations you saw coming; culture is what the team does in the ones you did not. Put a name against every AI-assisted deliverable, so a person still owns the judgment when the model produced the draft. A standard like that holds only if you keep to it yourself. *When Air Canada's bot invented a policy and a tribunal held the airline to it, the lesson is that the more hands touch a system, the easier it is for accountability to land on no one.*

None of this is about watching the work more closely. Each condition is what lets a team move at the speed AI offers without you in the loop on every call, and still hand you something you would put your own name on.

### {10} Build your leadership skill file

This is the module's hands-on exercise. Turn the judgment you have built across this course into a short set of instructions any AI tool can follow. Your goal is a portable skill file, in markdown, that works to your standards without you re-explaining yourself every time.

Every module so far has been an exercise in codifying judgment so it scales beyond the person holding it: strategy into an OKR cascade, ownership into a charter, a bet into kill criteria. A personal skill file is the AI-era version of that same discipline. It takes the leadership judgment living in your head, how you pressure-test a strategy, how you separate the loud from the strategic, how you price a bet and name its kill criterion, and encodes it into something portable. Pick the two or three areas where your point of view is strongest and go deep, add one or two stakeholders you regularly need to convince, then prompt a model to draft the file and refine it until it sounds like you. The full instructions and prompt are in the lab guide.

## 02 · Governing AI Bets

### {12} Build, buy, or partner on AI

Setting the conditions for how a team works with AI assumes the harder call was already made: that the bet was worth making at all. Everything you have learned about strategy returns to one question, what makes a position defensible enough to be worth building on, and an AI capability is where that question is hardest, because the same models, tools, and ability to build fast now reach every competitor at once. Build, buy, or partner is the downstream call. The one underneath is whether the capability can be made defensible at all.

- **Build.** Owning a capability is not the same as holding an advantage. If a competitor with the same models could assemble the same thing, you carried the full cost for none of the protection. Building defends you only when the inputs underneath (the data, talent, and context you have accumulated) are things someone starting today could not reproduce. If you cannot name those inputs, question the instinct to build.
- **Buy.** When a capability is table stakes, there is discipline in refusing to build what buys you no edge. The value of buying is speed, and speed becomes an advantage only when being early hardens into a position a later entrant cannot unwind: a data flywheel already turning, a customer default already set, a share lead that compounds. The test is whether you can name the position early movement wins, and whether a later buyer could take it back.
- **Partner.** The safe-looking middle carries the sharpest dependency: you build on a capability anyone can license on the same terms, following a roadmap you do not set. The capability itself can never be your moat, because it is rentable by definition. Whatever defends you comes from the integration, how deeply you wire it into your product, data, and workflow, so the combination gives customers something they could not get from the partner directly.

The path never creates the moat; you bring the moat to the path. Name the defensible thing before debating how to acquire it, because the honest answer usually settles which path was right anyway.

### {13} What AI adds to every path

Once you have chosen your path and named your moat, the question that remains is what the capability costs you to live with. These four are the ongoing weight it carries after you commit, the costs that keep moving instead of settling. A leader who priced only the upfront comparison meets each by surprise.

- **Infrastructure costs scale with usage.** Success is what makes this expensive. A usage-priced capability climbs with every win, so you plan for the cost of winning, not just the cost of launching.
- **Vendor dependency is a standing risk.** A provider can change pricing, deprecate a model, or shift its roadmap, and your product inherits that overnight. Know what breaks if the provider changes, and have somewhere else to go.
- **Maintenance is a permanent cost.** The model you launch on is not the model you will be running, because providers keep updating theirs and behavior drifts. A capability that cleared your bar at launch can slip below it later with no change on your side.
- **Data ownership is a strategic stake.** If your usage trains a provider's model, you are helping fund a capability your competitors can rent too. Decide up front what data leaves and what stays yours, because that line determines how much of the advantage you keep.

The bet you approve is never quite the bet you end up running. Pricing these in before you commit, rather than meeting them after, is what separates a bet you chose from one that chose you.

### {14} The same discipline, with harder terrain

You already know how to run an experiment: form a hypothesis, decide what proves it, give it a fair run, and stop it when the evidence says so. A strategic bet is the same shape at a leader's altitude, and the kill criteria you built in the financial case are the same instrument. With AI the four steps do not change. What changes is how hard each one is to hold once the bet is live.

- **Name the Bet.** The pull with AI is to fund the tool because it is impressive, so the discipline is to write the metric it has to move and the number that clears it. A bet you can only defend by demoing is one you have no way to end.
- **Define Success First.** A target set after you have seen the results is one the results will always clear, and AI invites exactly that, because it is unusually good at looking successful without moving anything. Set the threshold and read date in writing before launch.
- **Give It a Fair Run.** A model update, a price change, or a competitor's release can invalidate the case you approved partway through, so the fixed review window is where you re-check whether you would still fund this today.
- **Set the Kill Criteria.** AI bets rarely die on their own, because there is always a next version promising the thing that did not work is about to. Decide in advance the number that ends it and who has to agree, so the call is made before anyone falls in love with the roadmap.

A bet nobody will end is not a bet, it is a subscription to hope. The discipline is not new to you; what is new is needing it more, because AI is the easiest thing you will ever fund to start and the hardest to stop.

### {15} Responsible AI decisions

Governing a bet is also about whether you can stand behind what it does once it is live. Bias, trust, and explainability reach you as product questions long before they become legal or engineering ones, and the leaders who handle them well raise them while the bet is still on the table.

- **Trust.** A user burned by a single confident, wrong answer discounts every correct one that comes after. Whether the product catches that error or the user does is a design decision you make in advance. *The question you own: when it is confidently wrong, does the product catch it, or does the user?*
- **Explainability.** Knowing what the system produced is not the same as being able to trace why, and only the second lets you stand behind the decision when you are asked to. *The question you own: can we trace why it did what it did, or only that it did?*
- **Bias.** A model can clear every top-line metric and still fail the exact users your bet was meant to win, because the people it serves worst are usually least visible in the numbers you report. Break the metric out by segment before launch. *The question you own: who does this system work worse for, and would that show up in our metrics?*

None of these land on someone using AI to sharpen their own work. They arrive the moment AI becomes a bet the organization made on your judgment. The domain sets the stakes; the ownership stays yours.

## 03 · Final Project Showcase: Deliver Your Product Strategy

### {19} The learner journey

The step from managing a product to leading one, module by module:

- **Module 1 · The Strategic Leap.** From running the product you were handed to setting the direction the organization builds toward. The win: a Playing to Win cascade carried into OKRs, specific enough that a skeptical board cannot poke a hole in it.
- **Module 2 · The Focus Reframe.** From a backlog of everything to a clear point of view on what earns a place and what waits. The win: an outcome-based Now/Next/Later roadmap where every near-term bet traces back to strategy.
- **Module 3 · The Leadership Reset.** From hoping team problems resolve themselves to diagnosing the real cause and leading the conversation. The win: a way to name any situation as a Feedback, Clarity, System, or Hiring issue and open the conversation it calls for.
- **Module 4 · The Influence Edge.** From being right to bringing the room with you when priorities collide. The win: an executive communication that lands a hard call and keeps the relationship intact.
- **Module 5 · The Language Shift.** From defending your work on conviction to defending it in the language that funds it. The win: a business case with a kill criterion sharp enough for someone outside your team to enforce.
- **Module 6 · The Leverage Range.** From using AI on your own work to setting how a team and an organization build with it. The win: your leadership judgment encoded into a portable skill file any AI tool can apply.

### {20} Prepare and finalize your deliverables

Polish your content, verify your links work, and make your screenshots clear. The point is not perfection; it is your strategic logic and what you learned during the build. The five deliverables:

- **Strategy:** your Playing to Win cascade and OKRs, so the strategy reads as one connected argument.
- **Roadmap:** your Now/Next/Later outcome roadmap and trade-off memo, with every Now item tracing back to an OKR.
- **Team Charter:** What We Own and How We Decide, specific enough that a new PM would know exactly what they own and how a cross-team conflict gets resolved.
- **Financial Model:** your business case and a kill criterion specific enough that someone outside your team could enforce it.
- **Individual Insights:** your friction points, the key aha moment, and your overall takeaways from the course.
