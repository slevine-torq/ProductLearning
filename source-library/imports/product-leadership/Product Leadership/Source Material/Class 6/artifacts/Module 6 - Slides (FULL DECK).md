## Section 7 — The stages of AI leverage as a leader · FRAMEWORK

| Stage | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Personal Productivity** | You use AI to move faster on your own work | You turn a rough idea into a clear PRD draft in minutes instead of hours |
| **2 · Team Capability** | You set how the team adopts, checks, and trusts AI | Every PM writes PRDs to the same prompt, so quality holds and reviews move faster |
| **3 · Org Advantage** | You make bets on AI that compound across the org | PRDs feed a shared system that keeps the whole org building against one source of truth |

> **Your leadership lives in Stages 2 and 3.** The trap is running them on Stage 1 instincts: **a leader who stays the best AI user on the team quietly caps what the team can do at whatever they personally have time to model.**

## Section 8 — Where AI earns its place · Stage 2 in practice

*You have already run every one of these workflows for yourself, so you know what good looks like. **That judgment is the asset your team cannot borrow.** Stage 2 is turning it into something they can work from.*

1. **Strategy** — The quality of a team's strategic thinking usually depends on which PM ran the analysis. When the standard for pressure-testing a landscape is the same for everyone, the team's judgment holds together even on work you never see.
2. **Prioritization & Roadmapping** — A hard cut gets reopened the moment someone senior pushes on it, unless the reasoning was built to be defended by whoever holds the line that day.
3. **Development & Coaching** — *The one that stays closest to your own judgment.* Coaching a person through a hard conversation is not something you standardize and hand off; here AI turns scattered signals into themes so you arrive clearer. **Naming that honestly matters.**
4. **Stakeholder Alignment** — An uneven pre-read from one PM costs the whole team credibility others spent months earning.
5. **Financial Bets** — The gap in a funding conversation is between a PM who can *assemble* a business case and one who can *hold it up* when finance questions the load-bearing assumption.

## Section 9 — Leading a team that builds with AI · FRAMEWORK + REAL PUBLIC CASES

*Set these three well and you can step back, because a team that knows the rules does not need you reviewing its work to stay safe.*

| Condition | What it is | Action | Real-world failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · Norms** | Where the team leans on AI, where a human still signs off, and how that's communicated so nobody has to guess | Write the one-page rule for which tasks ship on AI output alone and which need human review | **Chevrolet's dealership bot** was talked into "selling" a car for a dollar. *The failure was not the jailbreak; no one had drawn the line between what the AI could say and what it could commit to.* |
| **2 · Risks** | The quiet failures: over-trust in a confident wrong answer, skills eroding as easy reps get automated, ownership blurring | Name the one skill you will not let AI absorb, and require the team to do those reps by hand | An **Aalto accounting team's** AI prompt produced good output for months, until the model changed and quality quietly slipped. *The team no longer caught it, because the skill to spot the miss had eroded.* |
| **3 · Culture** | The standard you hold to — a human always owning the judgment while AI speeds the path to it | Put one name against every AI-assisted deliverable, so a person owns the judgment even when the model did the draft | When **Air Canada's** bot invented a policy and a tribunal held the airline to it: *the more hands touch a system, the easier it is for accountability to land on no one.* |

## Section 11 — Build, buy, or partner on AI · FRAMEWORK

> **With AI, any path can get you the capability, because the same models and tools now reach every competitor at once. What separates them is which one earns you a moat.**

| Path | Choose it when | Your moat test |
|---|---|---|
| **Build** | The capability is close to what makes you different and worth owning outright | You should be able to name what you have — data, talent, or context — that a competitor starting today couldn't assemble |
| **Buy** | The capability is table stakes and moving now matters more than owning it | You should be able to name the advantage you win by moving first that a competitor buying the same tool later can't easily take back |
| **Partner** | Your edge is how you integrate depth you can't build in-house | You should be able to name what your integration gives customers that they couldn't get from the partner directly |

> **You bring the moat to the path, not the other way around.** Name the defensible thing before debating how to acquire it — the honest answer usually settles which path was right anyway.

## Section 12 — What AI adds to every path

*The ongoing weight the capability carries **after** you commit — costs that keep moving instead of settling.*

- **Infrastructure scales with usage** — every new user adds to the bill, so the more the product succeeds, the more it costs to run. **Plan for the cost of winning, not just the cost of launching.**
- **Vendor dependency is a standing risk** — your capability rises and falls with a provider's pricing, uptime, and roadmap, none of which you control.
- **Maintenance is a permanent cost** — models change as providers update them, so a capability that cleared your bar at launch can slip below it later **with no change on your side.**
- **Data ownership is a strategic stake** — if your usage trains a provider's model, you help fund a capability your competitors can rent too.

> **The bet you approve is never quite the bet you end up running.**

## Section 13 — The same discipline, with harder terrain

*The four steps don't change. What changes is how hard each is to hold once the bet is live.*

1. **Name the Bet** — write the metric the bet has to move and the number that clears it. **If the only thing you can point to is a working demo, you have a toy, not a bet.**
2. **Define Success First** — set the threshold and read date before launch, in writing. **A target you set after seeing results is one the results will always clear.**
3. **Give It a Fair Run** — fix the review window up front and re-check whether you'd still fund it today, because a model or price change can invalidate the case you approved.
4. **Set the Kill Criteria** — pre-commit the number that ends it *and who has to agree*, so the call is already made before the next version buys it another quarter.

> **A bet nobody will end is not a bet, it is a subscription to hope. AI is the easiest thing you will ever fund to start and the hardest to stop.**

## Section 14 — Responsible AI decisions · FRAMEWORK

*Bias, trust, and explainability reach you as **product** questions long before they become legal or engineering ones.*

| | Why it matters | **The question you own** |
|---|---|---|
| 🤝 **Trust** | One confident miss can cost you the adoption you spent the whole bet trying to earn | *When it is confidently wrong, does the product catch it, or does the user?* |
| 🔍 **Explainability** | A decision you cannot explain is one you cannot defend when the room asks why | *Can we trace why it did what it did, or only that it did?* |
| 👨‍⚖️ **Bias** | The bet you championed can quietly underperform for the users you were most trying to win | *Who does this system work worse for, and would that show up in our metrics?* |

> The domain sets the stakes; the ownership stays yours.

## Section 15 — LAB · 15 min · Build your leadership skill file

Turn the judgment built across the course into a short set of instructions any AI tool can follow — **a portable skill file, in markdown.**

1. **Gather your standards** — pick the two or three areas where your point of view is strongest; add a key stakeholder or two
2. **Prompt your tool**
3. **Read and refine** — until the file sounds like *you*, not generic advice
4. **Keep going** — a file you reuse and refine over time, not a deliverable you submit

> **Every module has been an exercise in codifying judgment so it scales beyond the person holding it:** strategy into an OKR cascade, ownership into a charter, a bet into kill criteria. **A skill file is the AI-era version of that same discipline.**

## Section 23 — Key takeaways: Module 6 in four lines

1. **AI's leverage stops being personal the moment it becomes a standard a whole team works to.** Leaders who set where it leads and where a human still decides raise the team's ceiling, not only their own.
2. **With AI, any competitor can acquire the same capability, so the capability is rarely the advantage.** Leaders who name what makes a bet defensible before they build, buy, or partner are the ones who hold an edge.
3. **An AI initiative is a bet like any other, but the easiest to start on excitement and the hardest to stop.** Setting the kill criterion before the work begins keeps a good demo from outliving its results.
4. **Bias, trust, and explainability are product decisions long before they become legal or engineering ones.** Leaders who ask who a system fails and whether they can explain it protect the trust their bet depends on.
