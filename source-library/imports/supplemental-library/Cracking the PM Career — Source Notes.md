## 2 — Frameworks in our own words

Written from the coverage map with sources closed.

### 2.1 The five skill areas

The book's spine. Rather than treating "PM skills" as one undifferentiated blob, it splits them into five groups that grow in different orders and at different rates:

| Skill area | What it covers | Book part |
|---|---|---|
| **Product skills** | Understanding users, reading data, structured problem solving, design judgement, technical grounding, writing things down | C |
| **Execution skills** | Running the project, scoping to something shippable, launching, and personal throughput | D |
| **Strategic skills** | Vision, strategy, roadmap, goals — deciding what *should* be built, not just building it well | E |
| **Leadership skills** | Mindset, collaboration, influence without authority, communication, mentoring, cross-department work | F |
| **People management skills** | Hiring, coaching, team design, and running an organization that works | G |

The consultant translation: these are **five separately-earned currencies**, not one score. Someone can be strong on execution and weak on strategy and still be genuinely good at their level. That's a more honest model for staffing than a single seniority number, and it's directly usable as a self-assessment axis in a Torq tier rubric.

### 2.2 Scope, autonomy, impact — the load-bearing framework

The book's central and most-repeated claim about levels: **your level is set by your scope, your autonomy, and your impact — not by a checklist of skills you can demonstrate.**

- **Scope** — how big and how ambiguous the territory you're responsible for.
- **Autonomy** — how little help you need to succeed inside it.
- **Impact** — what actually shipped and what it did.

And the mechanism that follows: you get to the next level by showing autonomy and impact **at your current scope**, which earns the trust to be handed a bigger one. You do not get there by accumulating skills off a rubric. Bavaro is fairly blunt that promotion follows from shipping successful things, not from skill-collecting.

The book also explicitly criticises conventional ladder rubrics: they're either too vague to act on ("demonstrates strong judgement") or so specific they get applied inconsistently, and they often just describe the scope someone was already assigned — which makes the rubric circular.

**Why this matters most for Torq.** The `Torq Consultant Learning Path.md` tiers already split on *what the client sees you produce and defend*, not years served. That is the same move this book makes, arrived at independently. Scope/autonomy/impact is a ready-made vocabulary for the tier definitions, and the rubric critique is a warning about how *not* to write them.

The three map cleanly onto the tiers already defined:

| | Scope | Autonomy | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1** — Associate / Sr. Associate | One artifact, one workstream slice, handed to them scoped | Needs the structure supplied; a template and a reviewer | The artifact exists, is right, and arrives on time |
| **Tier 2** — Consultant / Sr. Consultant | A workstream end to end, including its ambiguity | Works from a goal rather than a spec; defends the output in the room | The client changed a decision because of the work |
| **Tier 3** — Principal / AD / Director | The engagement, its shape, and its commercial terms | Sets the scope others work inside; is the one being negotiated with | The engagement got extended, or the client's operating model changed |

### 2.3 The three career phases — the closest thing to a ready-made tier model

Bavaro's most-repeated framing in interviews, and the single best fit for the existing three tiers:

**Phase 1 — Shipping products.** Given the context and the goal, can you reliably ship something customers like that hits the number? Research, design validation, testing, iteration. The whole game is execution quality.

**Phase 2 — Product strategy.** The step to Senior PM is a **job change, not a skill upgrade**. Bavaro is explicit and repeats it in multiple interviews: getting better at shipping does not get you to Senior PM; getting better at strategy does. The new work is deciding *what to pursue* — spotting opportunities, forming a view, setting direction rather than receiving it.

**Phase 3 — Organizational excellence.** Impact through the organization rather than through your own hands: hiring, coaching, and the processes that let a team produce good strategy and good shipping repeatedly. Bavaro adds a second half that's easy to miss — you are also now a strategic advisor to the company, sitting at the executive table on decisions beyond your own area.

**The mapping to Torq tiers is close to 1:1**, with one important adjustment:

| Book phase | Torq tier | Adjustment needed |
|---|---|---|
| Shipping products | Tier 1 | Clean fit. "Produces the artifact; someone else defends it" is phase 1 stated in consulting terms |
| Product strategy | Tier 2 | Clean fit, and it **sharpens** the existing Tier 2 definition. Tier 2's stated job is owning a workstream and surviving pushback — the book adds *why* that's a different job rather than a better version of Tier 1 |
| Organizational excellence | Tier 3 | **Partial fit — this is where the book stops being directly usable.** Phase 3 assumes you manage the people. A Torq Principal usually advises a client team they don't manage. The *advisor-to-executives* half of phase 3 transfers completely; the *hire, coach, run the org* half only applies to an AD managing Torq consultants |

That adjustment is not a new finding — `Torq Consultant Learning Path.md` §4 already makes exactly this call about Product Leadership M3, for the same reason. **The two sources agreeing independently is worth noting: the standard PM ladder's third rung is people management, and the consulting third rung is not.** A Torq tier model that borrows this book wholesale will import an assumption that doesn't hold.

### 2.4 Level-by-level

Bavaro's own enumeration of the ladder: **APM → PM → Senior PM → Principal PM → PM Lead / Director → Head of Product.** Two useful details:

- **Principal PM is conditional on the business, not just the person.** It exists as a senior individual-contributor track only where the company has problems big enough to need one. Directly relevant to Torq: whether a Principal Consultant role is real depends on whether engagements exist at that scope.
- **Senior PM is a legitimate place to stop.** Bavaro says plainly that a high-performing, independent Senior PM creates real value and that a company is happy to keep one indefinitely — someone can spend a whole career there by choice. Worth carrying into the Torq tier framing: **Tier 2 should be described as a destination, not a waiting room.** A tier model that reads as "everyone is en route to Tier 3" will misdescribe most of the population and demotivate the tier doing most of the client work.

### 2.5 Junior vs. senior, concretely

The distinctions the excerpts draw, which are the most directly usable material for tier-differentiated learning content:

| | More junior | More senior |
|---|---|---|
| **Optimisation frame** | Optimises for their own product | Optimises across products, and for the long term |
| **Structure** | Needs templates and preset process | Works without them; often supplies them for others |
| **Ambiguity** | Wants the question answered before starting | Comfortable starting with the question unsettled |
| **Trade-offs** | Escalates them | Makes them, and can defend the reasoning |
| **Direction** | Receives what to work on | Argues for what should be worked on |
| **Team** | Is coached | Empowers others and has earned decision-making trust |
| **Time horizon** | This launch | The next several |

### 2.6 Strategy structure

For Phase 2 / Tier 2, the book splits strategic thinking into three distinct artifacts, which is more useful than treating "strategy" as one thing:

1. **Vision** — an inspiring picture of the future state.
2. **Strategic framework** — the specific bets and pillars chosen to get there.
3. **Strategic roadmap** — the sequenced path.

The failure mode it's positioned against: shipping whatever customers most recently asked for, which produces motion without direction. For Torq this is close to what Product Leadership M1's cascade already covers — worth cross-checking rather than teaching twice.

### 2.7 Problem-solving tools

From the Analytical Problem Solving chapter — light, portable, and a good fit for Tier 1/2 content because they're artifact-shaped:

- **Eigenquestions** — find the one question that, once answered, settles most of the others. (Originates with Shishir Mehrotra; the book uses it rather than coining it. Tag Origin as Industry-standard, credit accordingly.)
- **2×2 matrices** — make a two-variable trade-off visible so people argue about the right axis.
- **Red/yellow/green tables** — compare several options against several criteria without pretending to a false total score.
- **Five Whys** — push past the first plausible cause. Industry-standard, from Toyota; not the authors'.
- **Structured brainstorming** — slice the problem space first, then generate inside each slice, so ideation doesn't cluster in one corner.

### 2.8 Leadership without authority

Chapter 21's topic, and the highest-value Tier 2 chapter in the book for Torq specifically — a consultant *never* has formal authority on a client team. The book treats influence without authority as a named, learnable skill rather than a personality trait. This runs parallel to Product Leadership M4, which `Torq Consultant Learning Path.md` §4 already assigns to Tier 2 as "the one PL module that works without authority." **Same conclusion, two independent sources — that's a strong signal for where to put weight.**

Bavaro adds a related move in interview: entering an organization with weak product practice, don't lead with criticism. Ask what the current process is and where it hurts, then introduce practice that answers the pain someone already named. For a consultant walking into a client org in week one, that is close to the whole job.

## 3 — What this feeds, and what it doesn't

### Feeds the tier model directly

1. **Scope / autonomy / impact** as the vocabulary for what separates the tiers — replacing anything that reads like a skill checklist.
2. **The three phases** as the shape of the progression, with the phase-3 adjustment in §2.3 applied.
3. **The junior/senior table in §2.5** as concrete, observable tier behaviours — the kind of thing a Torq manager could actually assess against.
4. **The five skill areas** as an assessment axis, so a consultant can be strong in one currency and developing in another without a single score.
5. **Senior PM as a terminal level** as an explicit framing choice for Tier 2.
6. **The rubric critique** as a guardrail on how the tier definitions get written.

### Does not feed it

- **Part I (Product Leader Q&A)** — eleven interviews about paths beyond PM (CPO, CEO, VC, angel, coaching, social impact). Interesting, not tier material.
- **PEARL / interview-structuring content** — already scoped out in the session prompts, which correctly separates interview prep from this program.
- **Most of Part G (People Management)** — assumes direct reports; only applies to an AD managing Torq consultants. Same call `Torq Consultant Learning Path.md` already made about PL M3.
- **Chapter 8, Technical Skills** — belongs to the *other* file, not this one. Reported to include a compact tour of APIs, deployment, SQL, and algorithms aimed at helping PMs partner with engineers and estimate cost. **This is a real cross-reference for the Technical Fluency Reference Guide** — and it covers deployment and SQL, two areas where *Tech Simplified* is strong, so the two corroborate on what a PM-level technical floor contains. Confidence: Medium, single search-surfaced description.
