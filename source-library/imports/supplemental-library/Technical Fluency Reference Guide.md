# Technical Fluency Reference Guide

**For Torq consultants — Associate through Director, including Enterprise Transformation PMs and program/project managers.**

The job of this guide is narrow and testable: **no Torq consultant should hear a technical term for the first time in a client room.** Not "learn to code." Not "become technical." Recognize the word, know roughly what it is, know why a team would use it, know where it shows up in a conversation, and have one real example to hang it on.

**Built:** 2026-08-27. **Plugs into:** [`Torq Consultant Learning Path.md`](Torq%20Consultant%20Learning%20Path.md) at all three tiers. **Method:** [`_LD-BUILD-METHOD.md`](PMC/Torq%20Lessons%20Build/Torq%20Rebuild/_LD-BUILD-METHOD.md). **Audience framing:** [`_TORQ-COMPANY-CONTEXT.md`](PMC/Torq%20Lessons%20Build/Torq%20Rebuild/_TORQ-COMPANY-CONTEXT.md).

## 1. How to use this

### The ceiling, stated up front

*Tech Simplified for PMs and Entrepreneurs* (Deepak Singh, 2022) opens with a five-level self-placement model, and it is the cleanest framing available for what this guide is trying to do. Reconstructed in [`Tech Simplified — Source Notes.md`](Tech%20Simplified%20—%20Source%20Notes.md):

| Level | What it means | Who it's for here |
|---|---|---|
| **L1** | No clue | Below Tier 1 |
| **L2** | Broad understanding — you recognize the terms and roughly what they do | **Tier 1 target** |
| **L3** | You understand what a technical choice implies for the product | **Tier 2 target** |
| **L4** | You understand each decision and its trade-offs in engineering detail | An engineer's bar. **No Torq tier needs this.** |
| **L5** | You write the code | Out of scope |

**Tier 3 is not L4.** A Principal or Director doesn't need deeper engineering detail than a Senior Consultant — they need the *same* L3 facts plus the commercial consequence: what a client's stated stack implies about delivery risk, hiring market, vendor lock-in, and whether the SOW estimate is credible. That's a different axis, not a deeper one. Every deep-dive table below is built on that assumption.

### Reading order by tier

| Tier | Roles | What to read | What you should be able to do afterward |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1** | Associate / Sr. Associate | **§2 master table only.** Read it once end to end, then keep it open during client standups. | Not be surprised. When someone says "we'll cron that job overnight," you know what was said and don't need to nod through it. |
| **Tier 2** | Consultant / Sr. Consultant | Master table **plus every category deep-dive (§3–§11)**, especially §4 Frontend, §5 Backend, §6 Data, §9 Delivery, §11 AI. Then §12 phrasebook. | Discuss a trade-off out loud. "You're on React Native — that buys you one team instead of two, and it costs you at the edges where you need real native performance." |
| **Tier 3** | Principal / AD / Director | Master table **plus the "Strategic & risk read" column** in every deep-dive. Skip the mechanics columns. Then §13 and §14. | Price the stack. Hear "we're on a Java monolith with no CI/CD" and immediately know what that does to the estimate, the hiring plan, and the phase-two pitch. |

### Where this sits in each tier's path

- **Tier 1** — before Claude Code For PM M1. Broad-exposure framing.
- **Tier 2** — alongside Claude Code For PM M4. CC4PM 4.1 asks you to read a real codebase and 4.2 to pair with engineering; both assume the vocabulary floor this guide supplies.
- **Tier 3** — reference and refresh. Master table plus risk columns, not the mechanics.

### One rule about how you use the vocabulary

Fluency is for *understanding and asking*, not for performing. The fastest way to lose an engineering room is to use a term slightly wrong with confidence. The move this guide is training is **"I know what that is — tell me why you chose it,"** not **"ah, of course, classic microservices play."** Recognition buys you the right to ask a good question. It does not buy you an opinion on their architecture.

> **Regulated client?** If you're engaged with a healthcare, aerospace & defense, or financial-services client, anything in this guide touching data movement, third-party APIs, cloud hosting, or AI tooling likely needs legal/compliance review, a longer approval cycle, and data-handling constraints before it can move forward the way a vendor's docs describe. Assume "we'll just call their API" and "we'll put that in the LLM's context" are both compliance questions before they're technical ones.

## 2. Master table — every term, one row

Sorted by category, then roughly by how often you'll hear it. Every example is a documented public case, not a scenario. Category tags: **Language · Frontend · Backend · Data Format · Data Store · Interface · Concept · Delivery · Infrastructure · AI/Agentic**.

| Term | Category | What it is | Why it's used | Where you'll hear it | Real, verifiable example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **HTML** | Language (markup) | The markup that defines a web page's *structure* — headings, paragraphs, links, form fields. Not a programming language; it has no logic. | It's the only thing browsers natively render. Everything else on the web sits on top of it. | Any conversation about a web page's content or accessibility | Tim Berners-Lee's first public description of HTML in 1991; still the substrate under every site — "View Source" in any browser shows it |
| **CSS** | Language (style) | The rules that control how HTML *looks* — color, spacing, layout, responsive behavior. Also not a programming language. | Separates appearance from structure, so a design change doesn't require rewriting content. | "That's just a CSS change" (sometimes true, sometimes very not) | CSS Zen Garden (Dave Shea, 2003) — one identical HTML file, hundreds of radically different designs, changing only CSS |
| **JavaScript** | Language | The language that runs inside the browser and makes pages interactive. Also runs on servers via Node.js. | It's the only language browsers execute natively, which is why it's everywhere. | Almost every frontend conversation | Standardized as ECMAScript; the base language under React, React Native, Angular, and Vue |
| **TypeScript** | Language | JavaScript with a type system layered on — you declare what shape your data is, and the compiler catches mismatches before they ship. | Catches a whole class of bugs at build time instead of in production; makes large codebases navigable. | "Is the codebase typed?" — a fast read on maturity | Created at Microsoft (2012); VS Code, Slack's desktop app, and much of the modern React ecosystem are written in it |
| **Python** | Language | A general-purpose language known for readability. Dominant in data, ML, scripting, and automation; also a common web backend language. | Fastest path from idea to working code; the entire data-science and AI library ecosystem lives here. | Data teams, ML teams, automation, "we'll script that" | Created by Guido van Rossum (1991). Dropbox's desktop client and Instagram's backend are both publicly documented Python systems |
| **Java** | Language | A mature, statically typed, enterprise-grade language that runs on the JVM. Verbose, extremely stable, deep tooling. | Decades of proven reliability at scale, a huge hiring pool, and enormous existing investment in banks, airlines, insurers, and healthcare. | Any large enterprise client's backend | Netflix built and open-sourced its microservices stack (Eureka, Zuul, Hystrix) on the JVM; Android apps were written in Java before Kotlin |
| **Swift** | Language | Apple's language for iOS, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS, and visionOS apps. | It's the supported, first-class path for building on Apple platforms — best performance, earliest access to new OS features. | Any conversation about a native iPhone app | Announced by Apple at WWDC 2014, open-sourced in 2015. Airbnb rebuilt its iOS app in native Swift after publicly sunsetting React Native in 2018 |
| **Kotlin** | Language | The modern language for Android, interoperable with Java. | Less boilerplate than Java, fewer null-pointer crashes, and Google's stated preference. | Android app conversations | Google announced Kotlin-first for Android development in 2019 |
| **SQL** | Language (query) | The language for asking questions of a relational database. "Give me every order over $500 from last quarter." | It's the universal interface to structured data — analysts, engineers, and BI tools all speak it. | Data teams, reporting, "can we pull that?" | Standardized since 1986; the query language for PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle, SQL Server, Snowflake, and BigQuery alike |
| **React** | Frontend | A JavaScript library for building user interfaces out of reusable components. The default choice for new web frontends. | Component reuse, a huge ecosystem, and the largest hiring pool in frontend. | Nearly every web frontend conversation | Built at Facebook, open-sourced 2013. Netflix's web and TV UIs are publicly documented React implementations |
| **React Native** | Frontend | React, but rendering real native mobile components instead of web ones — one codebase, iOS and Android. | One team and one codebase instead of two; most business logic written once. | Any cross-platform mobile decision | Shopify publicly committed to React Native for mobile in 2020. Airbnb went the other way, publishing a five-part explanation in 2018 of why it went back to native |
| **Bootstrap** | Frontend | A pre-built library of CSS styles and components — grids, buttons, forms, navbars — you drop in rather than design. | Gets a usable, responsive, consistent UI fast without a designer. Dominant in internal tools and admin panels. | Internal tools, admin dashboards, "why does this look generic?" | Created at Twitter as an internal style guide, open-sourced in 2011; became the reason a wave of 2010s sites looked interchangeable |
| **Angular / Vue** | Frontend | The two main alternatives to React. Angular is opinionated and enterprise-common; Vue is lighter-weight. | Angular's structure suits large teams that want one prescribed way to do things; Vue suits smaller teams that want less ceremony. | When a client's frontend *isn't* React | Angular is maintained by Google; Vue was created by Evan You in 2014 and is independently governed |
| **Django** | Backend | A Python web framework that ships with an admin interface, database layer, authentication, and security defaults built in. | "Batteries included" — you get a working, secure CRUD application very fast without assembling a stack yourself. | Python-shop backends, internal tools, content-heavy sites | Built at the Lawrence Journal-World newspaper, open-sourced 2005. Instagram's engineering team has publicly described running Django at very large scale |
| **Node.js / Express** | Backend | JavaScript running on the server. Express is the minimal framework most Node backends use. | One language across frontend and backend; excellent at handling many concurrent connections. | Startups, API layers, real-time features | Node was created in 2009; Express remains the most common Node web framework |
| **Spring / Spring Boot** | Backend | The dominant Java backend framework in enterprises. | Enormous ecosystem, deep enterprise integrations, and the framework most Java hires already know. | Banks, airlines, insurers, healthcare backends | The standard framework across Java-based enterprise systems; Netflix's open-sourced components integrate with it |
| **.NET / C#** | Backend | Microsoft's language and framework family for backend and desktop applications. | Deep integration with Microsoft's stack — Azure, Active Directory, Office — which many enterprises are already fully invested in. | Microsoft-shop clients | Stack Overflow itself ran publicly on .NET for years and documented its architecture |
| **JSON** | Data Format | A plain-text way of writing structured data — keys and values, nested. The format nearly every API sends and receives. | Human-readable, lightweight, and every language can parse it. Won the format war outright. | Any API, integration, or "what does the payload look like?" conversation | Specified by Douglas Crockford; standardized as ECMA-404 (2013) and RFC 8259 (2017). Stripe's and GitHub's APIs both return JSON |
| **XML** | Data Format | An older, heavier structured format using nested tags. | Still entrenched in enterprise, finance, and healthcare integrations where the standards predate JSON. | Legacy integrations, SOAP services, EDI-adjacent work | HL7 v2/v3 messaging in healthcare and SOAP web services across banking still run on XML |
| **CSV** | Data Format | Comma-separated values — a flat text table. | Universally supported, opens in Excel, and is therefore how business data actually moves between organizations. | "Can you just send me an export?" | The default export format in essentially every SaaS product |
| **YAML** | Data Format | An indentation-based config format, easier for humans to write than JSON. | Configuration files — CI/CD pipelines, Kubernetes, infrastructure definitions. | DevOps and deployment conversations | GitHub Actions workflows and Kubernetes manifests are YAML |
| **SQL vs. NoSQL database** | Data Store | SQL databases store data in strict tables with defined relationships. NoSQL stores flexible documents or key-value pairs without a fixed schema. | SQL for data with real relationships and correctness requirements (money, orders, patients). NoSQL for enormous scale, flexible shape, or very high write volume. | Any "what's the data model?" conversation | Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper — driven by shopping-cart availability requirements — led to DynamoDB and helped launch the NoSQL category |
| **Data warehouse** | Data Store | A separate database optimized for analysis rather than for running the product. | Running heavy analytical queries against the live production database will slow or break the product. | Analytics, BI, "why can't we just query prod?" | Snowflake, Google BigQuery, and Amazon Redshift are the three most common |
| **API** | Interface | A defined way for one system to ask another system to do something or hand over data — a contract with a specified request and response. | Lets systems integrate without knowing each other's internals, and lets teams ship independently. | Constantly. Every integration conversation. | Stripe's payments API is the canonical example — thousands of companies take payments without building payment infrastructure |
| **REST** | Interface | The dominant convention for web APIs: address things as URLs, act on them with standard HTTP verbs, get JSON back. | Simple, cacheable, universally understood, and it uses the web's existing machinery. | Any API conversation by default | Defined by Roy Fielding in his 2000 doctoral dissertation; now the default style for public web APIs |
| **GraphQL** | Interface | An API style where the *client* specifies exactly which fields it wants in one request. | Stops mobile clients from making six calls and over-fetching data they don't need. | Mobile-heavy products, complex frontends | Developed at Facebook in 2012 for its mobile apps, open-sourced 2015; GitHub's v4 API is GraphQL |
| **Webhook** | Interface | The reverse of an API call — instead of you asking repeatedly, the other system notifies you when something happens. | Real-time updates without constant polling. | Integrations, "how will we know when it's done?" | Stripe, GitHub, and Slack all deliver events to customer systems via webhooks |
| **HTTP / HTTPS** | Interface | The protocol browsers and servers use to talk. HTTPS is the encrypted version. | HTTPS is now effectively mandatory — browsers actively warn users away from unencrypted sites. | Security reviews, integration specs | Google began using HTTPS as a search ranking signal in 2014; Chrome began marking plain HTTP pages "Not secure" in 2018 |
| **DNS** | Interface | The system that translates a human name (gotorq.com) into a machine address. | Nobody can memorize IP addresses, and it lets you move servers without changing the name. | Launches, cutovers, outages | The October 2016 Dyn DNS attack took Twitter, Spotify, Reddit, and others offline without touching those companies' own systems |
| **CRUD** | Concept | The four operations any data-holding application performs: **C**reate, **R**ead, **U**pdate, **D**elete. | It's the complete vocabulary for what a user can do to a record — which makes it a permissions model and a scoping shortcut. | Scoping, permissions, "it's just a CRUD app" | Salesforce object permissions are literally checkboxes for Read, Create, Edit, and Delete per profile |
| **Cron job** | Concept | A task scheduled to run automatically at fixed times — nightly, hourly, the first of the month. | Automates recurring work with no human trigger: reports, billing runs, syncs, cleanups. | "We'll pick that up in the overnight job" | `cron` shipped in Version 7 Unix (1979); its scheduling syntax is still what AWS EventBridge Scheduler and GitHub Actions use today |
| **Frontend vs. backend** | Concept | Frontend is what the user sees and touches; backend is the server-side logic and data behind it. | It's a division of labor, and it's why a "small visual change" and a "small data change" can cost wildly different amounts. | Every estimation conversation | The single most load-bearing distinction in this guide |
| **Non-functional requirements (NFRs)** | Concept | The qualities a system must have — how fast, how many concurrent users, how much downtime is tolerable, how much data loss is acceptable — as distinct from what it does. | **This is where product decisions turn into cost.** "Real-time" and "consistent within a minute" describe the same feature and price completely differently. | Architecture reviews, scoping, sizing | HealthCare.gov's October 2013 launch failed on load, not features — the functionality existed; the concurrency requirement wasn't met |
| **Monolith vs. microservices** | Concept | A monolith is one deployable application. Microservices split it into many small services that deploy independently. | Microservices let teams ship without coordinating; the cost is far more operational complexity and a distributed system to debug. | Modernization and transformation engagements | Netflix's multi-year migration from a datacenter monolith to cloud microservices, completed and publicly documented in January 2016 |
| **Technical debt** | Concept | Shortcuts taken earlier that now slow every future change. Sometimes deliberate and rational; sometimes just accumulated. | Naming it turns "engineering is being slow" into a scoping conversation with a cost. | Every roadmap negotiation | Southwest Airlines' December 2022 meltdown — 16,700 cancelled flights — was publicly attributed to crew-scheduling systems that hadn't kept pace |
| **Caching** | Concept | Keeping frequently used answers somewhere fast instead of recomputing or re-fetching them. | The cheapest large performance win available, and usually the first one attempted. | Performance conversations | Facebook's "Scaling Memcache at Facebook" (2013) documented memcached as core infrastructure; Redis is the common modern equivalent |
| **Load balancing** | Concept | Distributing incoming traffic across many servers so no single one is overwhelmed. | It's how a system scales horizontally and survives the loss of any one machine. | Scale and reliability conversations | Standard in every cloud — AWS Elastic Load Balancing, Google Cloud Load Balancing |
| **Replication / redundancy** | Concept | Keeping multiple copies of data or capacity so a failure doesn't cause loss or downtime. | Availability and disaster recovery — the difference between an incident and a catastrophe. | DR planning, compliance reviews | Cloud providers structure this as availability zones and regions precisely so a single facility's failure isn't fatal |
| **Partitioning / sharding** | Concept | Splitting one enormous dataset across multiple machines. | Past a certain size, no single database server is big enough. | Very-large-scale data conversations | A standard technique at consumer-internet scale; adds significant complexity to every query that crosses shards |
| **Dev / staging / production** | Concept | Three separate copies of the system: one to build in, one to test in, one that real users touch. | Prevents unfinished work from reaching customers, and gives you a realistic place to test. | Release planning, "can we test that?" | Near-universal practice; it's why "it works on my machine" and "we can't test that in prod" are real constraints, not excuses |
| **IDE** | Delivery | Integrated Development Environment — the application an engineer actually writes code in, with autocomplete, debugging, and version control built in. | Everything in one place. Choice of IDE is also sometimes forced by platform. | Watching an engineer's screen; onboarding conversations | VS Code has ranked as the most-used editor in Stack Overflow's Developer Survey for years running. **Xcode is required to build and submit iOS apps** — that's a constraint, not a preference |
| **Git / version control** | Delivery | A complete history of every change to the codebase, with the ability to branch, merge, and roll back. | It's how multiple people work on the same code without overwriting each other, and it's what makes "just revert it" possible — sometimes. | Every engineering team, always | Created by Linus Torvalds in 2005 to manage Linux kernel development |
| **GitHub / GitLab / Bitbucket** | Delivery | Hosted homes for Git repositories, plus code review, issue tracking, and automation. | Code review and CI run here; it's where engineering work is actually visible. | "It's in the repo," "open a PR" | GitHub hosts the majority of open-source software; GitLab and Bitbucket are common in enterprises with specific compliance needs |
| **CI/CD** | Delivery | Continuous Integration / Continuous Delivery — automated build, test, and release every time code changes. | Catches breakage in minutes instead of weeks, and makes releasing routine instead of an event. | **Ask about this in week one.** It is the ceiling on how fast the roadmap can actually move. | Knight Capital lost roughly $440M in 45 minutes in August 2012 on a botched manual deployment to eight servers |
| **Feature flag** | Delivery | A switch that turns a feature on or off for specific users without deploying new code. | Decouples "shipped" from "released" — enables gradual rollout, instant rollback, and A/B tests. | Launch planning, experimentation | Facebook's Gatekeeper is the well-known internal example; LaunchDarkly built a business on the pattern |
| **Observability / monitoring** | Delivery | Logs, metrics, traces, and alerting — how a team knows what the system is doing and that it broke. | Without it, the first person to know about an outage is the customer. | Incident reviews, operational readiness | Datadog, Splunk, and New Relic are the common commercial tools |
| **Cloud computing** | Infrastructure | Renting compute, storage, and services from AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud instead of owning servers. | Capacity on demand, no datacenter to run, and managed services instead of building your own. | Nearly every infrastructure conversation | Netflix completed its move off its own datacenters onto AWS in January 2016 and published the account |
| **Infrastructure as code (IaC)** | Infrastructure | Describing servers, networks, and services in files instead of clicking through a console. | Infrastructure becomes versioned, reviewable, and rebuildable — and identical across environments. | Platform teams, environment setup | HashiCorp's Terraform (2014) and AWS CloudFormation are the standard tools |
| **Container / Docker** | Infrastructure | Packaging an application with everything it needs to run, so it behaves identically everywhere. | Kills "works on my machine" and makes deployment uniform. | Deployment and modernization conversations | Docker's 2013 release made the pattern mainstream |
| **Kubernetes** | Infrastructure | The system that runs, scales, and restarts large fleets of containers. | Automates operating many services at scale — and brings real operational complexity of its own. | Platform teams, "are we on K8s?" | Built at Google, open-sourced 2014, now governed by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation |
| **CDN** | Infrastructure | A network of servers worldwide that serve copies of your content from near the user. | Speed for global users, and absorbs traffic spikes and attacks. | Performance and global-launch conversations | Akamai, Cloudflare, and AWS CloudFront |
| **Open source** | Infrastructure | Software whose code is public and reusable under a license. Free to use is not the same as free of obligation. | Enormous leverage — you don't rebuild what exists. The license and the maintenance burden come with it. | Build/buy decisions, legal review | Log4Shell (December 2021) — a vulnerability in a widely used open-source Java logging library — forced emergency patching across essentially the entire enterprise Java world |
| **OAuth / SSO** | Infrastructure | Standards for logging in through an identity provider rather than a per-app password. | Central control of access, one identity, and removes password handling from each app. | Enterprise integration, security review | "Sign in with Google/Apple" is OAuth; Okta and Microsoft Entra ID are the common enterprise SSO providers |
| **LLM** | AI/Agentic | Large Language Model — a model trained on very large text corpora that generates text by predicting what comes next. | Handles language tasks (summarize, extract, classify, draft, converse) that previously required either a human or a bespoke model. | Every AI conversation in 2026 | Claude, GPT, and Gemini are the three families most enterprises evaluate |
| **Token / context window** | AI/Agentic | Text is split into tokens; the context window is how much the model can consider at once. | It's the unit of both cost and capacity — and the reason "just feed it all our documents" isn't a plan. | AI scoping and cost conversations | API pricing across every major provider is quoted per million input and output tokens |
| **RAG** | AI/Agentic | Retrieval-Augmented Generation — fetch relevant documents first, then give them to the model as context so its answer is grounded in your data. | The standard way to make a general model answer accurately about your specific business. | Every "can it use our data?" conversation | The term and technique come from a 2020 research paper by Lewis et al. (then Facebook AI Research) |
| **Embedding / vector database** | AI/Agentic | An embedding turns text into numbers capturing meaning; a vector database stores them so you can search by meaning rather than keyword. | It's the retrieval half of RAG — how the system finds relevant material when the wording doesn't match. | AI architecture conversations | Pinecone, Weaviate, and PostgreSQL's pgvector extension are common choices |
| **Fine-tuning** | AI/Agentic | Further training a model on your own examples to change its behavior or style. | Useful for consistent format or tone. Usually *not* the right answer for "it doesn't know our facts" — that's RAG. | Build/buy/tune decisions | Every major provider offers it; the build/buy/tune choice is a Tier 3 scoping decision |
| **Agent** | AI/Agentic | A system where the model decides which tools to call, in what order, to complete a multi-step task — rather than just answering. | Automates work that requires several steps and judgment between them. | 2026's most-oversold and most-consequential term | Claude Code is a working example — it reads files, runs commands, and edits code in a loop toward a stated goal |
| **MCP** | AI/Agentic | Model Context Protocol — an open standard for connecting AI models to external tools and data sources. One integration per tool instead of one per model-tool pair. | Solves the N×M integration problem. It's why a client's "should we build custom AI integrations?" question has a different answer now than in 2023. | Any AI tooling or integration roadmap | Introduced and open-sourced by Anthropic in November 2024; adopted by OpenAI in March 2025 and subsequently by other major providers |
| **Evals** | AI/Agentic | A structured test suite for an AI feature — known inputs, expected outputs, a measured pass rate. | It's the only way to know whether a prompt or model change made things better or worse. Vibes don't survive a client readout. | AI quality and governance conversations | The direct analogue of automated testing for conventional software; every serious AI team maintains one |
| **Hallucination** | AI/Agentic | A model producing confident, fluent output that is factually wrong. | It's the core reliability risk in every AI feature, and it's a design problem, not just a model problem. | Risk conversations, AI UX reviews | The reason AI-native UX emphasizes citations, confidence cues, and escape hatches |
| **AI coding assistant** | AI/Agentic | Tools that write, explain, and modify code alongside an engineer — or increasingly, on their own. | Shifts engineering effort from writing toward specifying and verifying. | Delivery estimates, team-shape conversations | GitHub Copilot (2021) started the category; Claude Code and Cursor are current agentic examples |

## 3. Deep dive — Languages

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** a language is a choice with a hiring market attached. HTML and CSS are *not* programming languages — they describe structure and appearance, not logic — and calling them languages in the wrong room is a small tell.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HTML** | Declarative markup, standardized by the WHATWG. Semantic HTML (using the *right* element for the job) is what screen readers and search engines depend on. | Accessibility and SEO are largely determined at the HTML level, not the visual level. A beautiful page built from generic containers can be unusable with a screen reader. | Accessibility is a legal exposure in several client industries and a procurement requirement in public-sector work. If a client's product has no accessibility audit, that's a finding, not a nice-to-have. |
| **CSS** | Cascading rules with specificity and inheritance. Modern layout is Flexbox and Grid; design systems are usually expressed as CSS variables (tokens). | "It's just CSS" is true for a color change and false for a layout change on a site with no design system — that becomes an audit of every page. Ask whether a design system exists before agreeing to a visual-change estimate. | A design system is a capital asset. Its absence is a recurring tax on every future UI change, and it's a legitimate line item in a transformation business case. |
| **JavaScript** | Single-threaded with an event loop; asynchronous by nature. Runs in browsers and, via Node.js, on servers. | Its ubiquity is the argument for it and against it — one language everywhere means simpler hiring and shared code, but JavaScript on the backend isn't automatically the right tool for heavy computation. | The largest developer population of any language. Low hiring risk; high variance in code quality, because the barrier to entry is low. |
| **TypeScript** | A compile-time type layer over JavaScript; the types disappear at runtime. | Untyped JavaScript in a large codebase is a real velocity drag. Adding types later is possible but is its own project — worth scoping honestly rather than assuming it's incremental. | "Is the codebase typed?" is one of the fastest reads available on engineering maturity, and it correlates with how safely a new team can take over after Torq leaves — which matters for handoff. |
| **Python** | Interpreted, dynamically typed, huge standard library. Owns data science and ML through pandas, NumPy, PyTorch, and scikit-learn. | Superb for speed of development and anything data or AI adjacent. Slower at raw computation than Java or Go, which rarely matters and occasionally matters enormously. | If a client is doing anything serious in data or AI, Python is in the stack. That has hiring, security-scanning, and dependency-management implications the client may not have planned for. |
| **Java** | Statically typed, compiled to bytecode, runs on the JVM. Verbose by design; extremely mature tooling and libraries. | Enterprise Java is stable and well-staffed, but a large Java monolith is usually where the technical debt lives. Modernizing it is a multi-year program, not a refactor. | This is the language under most Fortune 500 core systems in Torq's industries — banking, airlines, insurance, healthcare. Deep hiring pool, decades of proven operation, and an enormous replacement cost. "Rewrite it" is almost never the credible recommendation. |
| **Swift** | Compiled, modern, memory-safe, with SwiftUI as the current UI framework. Requires macOS hardware and Xcode to build. | Native Swift gets you the best performance and same-day access to new iOS capabilities. The cost is a separate codebase and a separate team from Android. | Swift means a Mac-hardware requirement, an Apple developer account, and App Store review in your release timeline. All three are schedule risks that don't exist for a web deliverable — and App Store review has no SLA you control. |
| **Kotlin** | Statically typed, fully interoperable with Java, dramatically less boilerplate. Google's preferred Android language since 2019. | A Java Android codebase can migrate to Kotlin file by file. That makes it one of the rare modernizations you can genuinely do incrementally. | Lower risk than most language migrations precisely because it's incremental. If a client cites Kotlin migration as a reason for a slow roadmap, that's worth probing. |
| **SQL** | Declarative — you describe the result you want, and the engine plans how to get it. Dialects differ between vendors but the core is portable. | The single highest-ROI technical skill for a PM or consultant. Being able to write your own query removes a dependency on the data team for every question you have. | A client whose business questions all route through a two-week data-team queue has an organizational problem the tooling can partly solve. That's a transformation finding. |

## 4. Deep dive — Frontend

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** frontend is what the user sees. React is the default; React Native is React for mobile; Bootstrap is pre-built styling; CSS is the underlying styling language. If a client says "we're a React shop," they've told you their hiring market and their component reuse story in three words.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **React** | Component-based, declarative. State changes trigger re-renders of just what changed. Usually paired with a framework like Next.js for routing and server rendering. | React is a *library*, not a full framework — teams assemble their own stack around it (routing, state, data fetching, forms). That flexibility is why two React codebases can look nothing alike, and why "we know React" doesn't mean an engineer knows *this* React codebase. | Lowest hiring risk in frontend by a wide margin, and the safest default recommendation. The real risk isn't React — it's the bespoke assembly of libraries around it, which is where lock-in and onboarding cost actually accumulate. |
| **React Native** | Renders genuine native UI components, not a web view. Business logic is shared; platform-specific behavior still needs platform-specific code. | The honest trade: one team and roughly 70–90% shared code, versus edge-case pain in animation, gestures, deep OS integration, and debugging across the JS/native boundary. **Both public outcomes are real** — Shopify committed to it in 2020 and published the reasoning; Airbnb abandoned it in 2018 and published a five-part explanation of why. Neither was wrong; they had different requirements. | The strategic question is never "is React Native good." It's "does this product's differentiation live in native platform capability?" If yes, cross-platform costs you the thing you're selling. If no, you're funding two mobile teams for no strategic return. That's a headcount decision worth several million dollars over an engagement's life. |
| **Bootstrap** | A CSS framework with a 12-column responsive grid and pre-styled components. Dropped in via a stylesheet. | Enormously fast for internal tools and admin panels where nobody needs a distinctive look. For a customer-facing brand experience, it's the wrong tool — you'll spend more overriding it than you saved. | Bootstrap in a *customer-facing* product is a signal worth investigating: it usually means the product was built without design involvement. That's often the actual root cause behind a client's "our UX is bad" complaint. |
| **CSS** | The cascade, specificity, and inheritance. Modern practice is Flexbox/Grid layout plus a token-based design system, often via Tailwind or CSS-in-JS. | The presence or absence of a design system is the single biggest driver of what a UI change costs. With one, a brand refresh is a token change. Without one, it's an audit of every screen. Ask early. | Design-system investment is a real capital-expenditure argument with a measurable return: every subsequent UI change gets cheaper. It's one of the more defensible line items in a digital-transformation business case. |
| **Angular / Vue** | Angular is a full opinionated framework (routing, forms, HTTP, DI all included) using TypeScript. Vue sits in between — more structure than React, less than Angular. | Angular's prescriptiveness is a genuine advantage on large teams that want one right way to do things. The cost is a steeper learning curve and a smaller hiring pool than React. | A client on Angular is not a problem. A client on **AngularJS** (the discontinued 1.x line, end-of-life since December 2021) is running unsupported software, which is a security and compliance finding, not a preference. Confirm which one they mean — the names are dangerously similar. |
| **Progressive Web App (PWA)** | A website that can be installed, work offline, and send notifications — without an app store. | A genuine third option in the native-vs-cross-platform debate: no app store review, instant updates, one codebase. The cost is reduced access to device capabilities and weaker discovery. | Skips App Store review and its revenue share entirely. Worth raising in any mobile scoping conversation where the client's main driver is release speed rather than device capability. |

## 5. Deep dive — Backend

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** the backend is the logic and data behind the screen. A framework is a pre-built structure so the team doesn't write authentication, routing, and database plumbing from scratch. Django is the Python one; Spring is the Java one; Express is the JavaScript one; .NET is the Microsoft one.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Django** | Batteries-included: ORM, migrations, auth, and an auto-generated admin interface out of the box. Follows a model-view-template structure with security defaults on. | The admin interface alone can eliminate weeks of internal-tool work — worth knowing about before you scope "we need a back-office screen." The trade-off is that Django is opinionated; fighting its conventions is expensive. | Fast to a working product, backed by a large Python hiring pool, and proven at scale — Instagram's engineering team has publicly described running Django at very large volume. The Python dependency chain does become a security-scanning obligation in regulated environments. |
| **Node.js / Express** | Event-driven and non-blocking; excellent at many concurrent I/O-bound connections. Express is minimal by design, so teams assemble the rest. | One language across the whole stack simplifies hiring and enables shared code. But minimal frameworks mean every team invents its own structure — onboarding a new engineer into someone's bespoke Express app is slower than it sounds. | Common in startups and in API/BFF layers in front of legacy systems. That "thin layer in front of the mainframe" pattern is frequently the pragmatic modernization play — and it's a much more credible recommendation than a rewrite. |
| **Spring / Spring Boot** | Dependency injection, convention-heavy configuration, and deep integration with enterprise infrastructure. Spring Boot removed most of the historical configuration burden. | Verbose and ceremonious, but the ecosystem covers essentially every enterprise need, and the hiring pool is deep and experienced. Rarely the wrong choice for a large regulated organization. | This is what's actually running under most of Torq's client industries. It is not a modernization target in itself — the modernization target is usually the *monolithic architecture* around it and the absence of CI/CD, not the framework. Get that distinction right in a scoping conversation. |
| **.NET / C#** | Cross-platform since .NET Core; strong typing, excellent tooling, tight Azure and Active Directory integration. | If a client is already committed to Microsoft — Azure, Entra ID, Office, Dynamics — .NET reduces integration friction substantially. Against that, it deepens an already-deep single-vendor relationship. | The vendor-concentration conversation. Microsoft-shop clients get real efficiency and real lock-in in the same purchase. Name both when a client asks whether to standardize further. |
| **ORM** | Object-Relational Mapper — lets engineers work with database rows as objects in their language instead of writing SQL by hand. | Removes enormous boilerplate and prevents whole classes of injection bugs. It also hides what queries are actually running, which is the usual root cause of "the app got slow as data grew." | When a client reports performance degrading with scale, the ORM's generated queries are a common and cheap-to-investigate culprit. Worth naming as a diagnostic step before anyone proposes re-architecture. |

## 6. Deep dive — Data formats, interfaces, and stores

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** JSON is how systems send structured data to each other. An API is the contract for asking another system for something. When someone says "we'll just call their API," the plan may or may not exist yet.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **JSON** | Six types: string, number, boolean, null, array, object. No comments, no date type, no schema of its own — JSON Schema is a separate standard. | Being able to read a JSON payload and see whether the field you need is actually in it is a genuine working skill. It converts "can we show the customer's tier on this screen?" from a two-day question into a two-minute one. | The absence of a documented schema across a client's integrations is a real handoff risk. Undocumented payloads are how integrations break silently after the consultant leaves. |
| **XML** | Verbose, nested, with a formal schema language (XSD) and namespaces. Heavier than JSON but far more rigorously specifiable. | Nobody chooses XML for a new project. Its presence tells you an integration is old and probably standards-mandated — and that changing it involves a partner, not just a sprint. | XML in healthcare (HL7) or finance is usually a regulatory or industry-standard obligation, not a technical choice. Estimating those integrations as if they're modern REST work is a classic scoping error. |
| **API** | Defines endpoints, request format, response format, authentication, rate limits, and versioning. | **"We'll just call their API" is a scoping claim with four hidden questions:** does it expose the data we need, at what rate limit, with what authentication, and who owns the credentials? Ask all four before that assumption reaches an estimate. | Third-party API dependency is vendor risk with a delivery date attached. Rate limits, pricing changes, deprecations, and outages are all outside your control and inside your timeline. It belongs in the risk register by name. |
| **REST** | Resource-oriented URLs plus HTTP verbs: GET reads, POST creates, PUT/PATCH updates, DELETE removes. Stateless, cacheable. | Note the mapping — REST's verbs *are* CRUD. Once you see that, most API documentation becomes readable without an engineer translating. | The default, well-understood, and lowest-risk choice. If a client has built something bespoke instead, ask why — the answer is either a real constraint or accumulated debt. |
| **GraphQL** | A single endpoint with a typed schema; clients specify exactly which fields they want. | Solves genuine over-fetching problems for mobile and complex UIs. The costs are real: caching is harder, and one badly written client query can put unexpected load on the backend. | Adds a capability layer and an operational responsibility. Justified for a mobile-heavy product with many clients; over-engineering for a single web app. |
| **Webhook** | The provider POSTs an event to a URL you host. Requires you to expose a public endpoint, verify signatures, and handle duplicate deliveries. | Far more efficient than polling, but you have to build for retries and duplicates — webhooks can and do arrive twice. That's a requirement, not an edge case. | An inbound public endpoint is an attack surface and a security-review item. In regulated environments it usually needs its own approval. |
| **SQL vs. NoSQL** | SQL enforces schema and transactional guarantees (ACID). NoSQL relaxes those for scale and flexibility, typically offering eventual consistency. | The decision usually turns on one question: does the data have real relationships and correctness requirements? Money, orders, patients, and bookings say SQL. Event streams, catalogs, and session data can say NoSQL. Most systems legitimately use both. | "Eventually consistent" is a business decision disguised as a technical one. In finance or healthcare it can be a compliance problem. Make sure a client's PM knows which one they picked and what it means. |
| **Data warehouse** | Column-oriented storage optimized for analytical queries over huge datasets, loaded from production systems on a schedule. | Explains why the dashboard is "as of last night" — the load job is typically a nightly cron. That's a design choice you can change, at a cost, if the business genuinely needs it fresher. | "We want real-time analytics" is one of the most expensive sentences in a scoping conversation. Establish what refresh interval the business decision actually requires before anyone prices streaming infrastructure. |

## 7. Deep dive — Core concepts

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** CRUD is the four things you can do to a record. A cron job is something the computer does on a schedule without being asked. NFRs are the "how well," not the "what."

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CRUD** | Create, Read, Update, Delete — the four persistent-storage operations, commonly credited to James Martin's 1983 work on database environments. Maps directly onto REST verbs and onto database statements. | Two working uses. **Permissions:** running CRUD against every role catches gaps a requirements doc misses — who can *delete*, and is it a real delete or a soft delete? **Scoping:** "it's just a CRUD app" is a real and useful estimate signal, and it's also frequently wrong once workflow, approvals, and audit requirements appear. | "Just a CRUD app" as a *client's* characterization is worth testing directly. In regulated industries, delete is almost never a real delete — retention and auditability requirements mean it's a status change, and that difference changes the data model and the estimate. |
| **Cron job** | A time-based scheduler using a five-field expression (minute, hour, day-of-month, month, day-of-week). From Version 7 Unix, 1979; the same syntax survives in AWS EventBridge Scheduler and GitHub Actions today. | Cron jobs are where invisible business logic hides. Nightly reconciliation, billing runs, data syncs, report generation, cleanup. **When mapping a client's system, explicitly ask what runs on a schedule** — it's rarely in the documentation and it's frequently load-bearing. | Scheduled batch jobs are a classic single point of failure with no user-visible symptom until something's already wrong. They also cap how fresh any downstream number can be. Both belong in a transformation assessment. A client whose critical processes are a chain of undocumented cron jobs has a continuity risk, not a tooling preference. |
| **Frontend / backend split** | Different languages, different skills, often different teams, and frequently different release cadences. | This is the answer to "why does this tiny change cost so much?" A visual tweak may be frontend-only. Adding one field to a screen can mean a database migration, an API version, a backend change, a frontend change, and a coordinated release. **Learning to predict which one you're asking for is the highest-value skill in this guide.** | Team topology follows this split, and so does the estimate. When a client's frontend and backend teams release on different cadences, every cross-cutting feature carries coordination cost that estimates routinely omit. |
| **NFRs** | Performance, scalability, availability, security, maintainability, accessibility, compliance. Distinct from functional requirements, which describe what the system does. | The most useful question in this entire guide is **"what are the non-functional requirements?"** — because a client's PM often hasn't been asked. It reframes vague ambition into priced options: 99.9% availability and 99.99% availability are the same feature at very different costs. | HealthCare.gov (October 2013) is the canonical case: the functionality shipped, the concurrency requirement didn't. NFRs are where scope turns into money, and where an SOW estimate is most often wrong in a direction you'll be blamed for. Get them in writing before the number is committed. |
| **Monolith vs. microservices** | Monolith: one deployable unit, simple to operate, coupled to release. Microservices: many independently deployable units, independent releases, and a distributed system to debug. | Microservices are an *organizational* solution before they're a technical one — they let teams ship without coordinating. Adopting them without the operational maturity (CI/CD, observability, on-call) produces a distributed monolith: all the complexity, none of the independence. | Netflix's migration took roughly eight years and finished in January 2016. A client proposing a similar move on an eighteen-month program is describing an ambition, not a plan. This is where a credible Tier 3 pushback earns the next phase of work. |
| **Technical debt** | Deliberate debt (a conscious shortcut with a known payoff) versus accidental debt (accumulated drift). The two need different responses. | Making debt visible is a PM job, not an engineering complaint. Naming a specific piece of debt, its cost per sprint, and its remediation cost converts an argument into a prioritization decision. | Southwest Airlines' December 2022 disruption — around 16,700 cancelled flights, roughly $750M–$1.1B in reported financial impact — was publicly attributed to crew-scheduling systems that hadn't kept pace with the operation. That's the number to use when a client's executive asks why modernization can't wait another year. |
| **Caching** | Multiple layers: browser, CDN, application, database. Each has its own invalidation rules. | Caching is the cheapest performance win and the source of the most confusing bugs. "It works for me but not for them" and "the old price is still showing" are usually cache invalidation. Ask about caching before anyone concludes the data is wrong. | Stale data has compliance consequences in pricing, healthcare, and financial disclosure. A cache TTL is a business decision in those contexts, not an implementation detail. |
| **Scaling levers** | Four, from the system-design literature: **partitioning** (split the data), **caching** (keep hot answers close), **redundancy** (spare capacity), **replication** (multiple copies). | These four are the whole vocabulary of a "will it scale?" conversation. Being able to ask *which* lever a team is pulling and what it costs is a credible engineering-room question. | Each lever costs money and complexity. "Will it scale" without a target number is unanswerable. Insist on the number — expected concurrent users, peak transactions per second — before anyone estimates. |
| **Dev / staging / production** | Three environments. Staging should mirror production; it usually doesn't, which is where the surprises live. | Ask directly: how close is staging to production, and does it have realistic data? A staging environment with 100 rows tells you nothing about performance with 100 million. That gap is where launch failures come from. | Environment parity is a leading indicator of delivery predictability. In regulated industries, production data cannot simply be copied to staging — synthetic or masked test data is a real, frequently unbudgeted work item. |

## 8. Deep dive — Networking and protocols

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** the postal-service analogy holds. DNS is the address book, IP is the address, TCP makes sure the package arrives complete and in order, and HTTP is the language written on the form inside. HTTPS means the envelope is sealed.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HTTP / HTTPS** | Request/response with methods, status codes, and headers. HTTPS wraps it in TLS encryption. Status codes are worth knowing: 200 OK, 301 redirect, 401 unauthenticated, 403 forbidden, 404 missing, 500 server error. | Reading status codes off a browser's network tab lets you tell "the API is down" from "we're not authorized" from "the URL is wrong" without waiting for an engineer. That's a real time saver in an incident. | Certificate expiry is a recurring, entirely preventable, entirely embarrassing outage cause across the industry. Ask whether renewal is automated. |
| **TCP / IP** | IP addresses and routes; TCP guarantees ordered, complete delivery with retries. | Explains latency as a physical constraint. A round trip between continents has a floor set by the speed of light — that's why a CDN and regional deployment exist. | Data residency and regional deployment are compliance requirements in the EU and increasingly elsewhere. "Where does the data physically live?" is a legal question with an infrastructure answer. |
| **DNS** | Maps names to addresses, with TTL-controlled caching at every layer in between. | DNS TTL is why a cutover isn't instant. Planning a launch or migration without accounting for DNS propagation is a common and avoidable failure. | The October 2016 Dyn attack knocked Twitter, Spotify, and Reddit offline without touching any of their own systems. DNS is a single point of failure most risk registers omit entirely. |
| **Encryption (at rest / in transit)** | In transit is TLS on the wire; at rest is encrypted storage. They're separate controls and both are usually required. | These are two different checkboxes, and clients routinely conflate them. Knowing the difference makes you useful in a security review rather than a spectator. | Effectively mandatory in every regulated Torq industry — HIPAA, PCI DSS, SOX-adjacent controls. Absence is a finding with a regulatory consequence, not a backlog item. |
| **VPN** | An encrypted tunnel making a remote machine appear to be on a private network. | Often the reason an integration works in the client's office and not from your laptop. Worth checking before diagnosing anything else. | VPN access for consultants is a real onboarding lead time — frequently one to three weeks at large enterprises. Build it into the engagement plan or lose the first sprint to it. |
| **OAuth / SSO** | OAuth 2.0 issues scoped tokens so an app acts on a user's behalf without ever seeing their password. SSO centralizes identity through a provider. | Integrating with enterprise SSO is almost always more work than estimated — provider quirks, group mapping, and provisioning all take longer than the happy path suggests. | Identity is where enterprise integration projects most reliably slip. If a deliverable requires SSO integration, treat the client's identity team as a critical-path dependency from day one. |

## 9. Deep dive — Delivery and tooling

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** an IDE is where code gets written. Git is the history of every change. CI/CD is the automation that builds, tests, and releases it. Ask about CI/CD in week one — it tells you how fast this team can actually move.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IDE** | Editor plus compiler integration, debugger, test runner, and version control in one application. Some are optional; some are mandatory. | Worth knowing which are forced: **Xcode is required to build and submit iOS apps**, and Android Studio is the practical requirement for Android. Those aren't preferences, they're constraints on hardware and licensing. | AI-assisted IDEs (Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code) are now a procurement and policy question, not a developer preference. Client policy on what code may be sent to a third-party model is a live constraint on your own delivery team — confirm it before the engagement starts, per Torq's AI-and-data policy. |
| **Git / version control** | Distributed history, branching, merging, pull requests. Branching strategy (trunk-based vs. long-lived feature branches) shapes how a team works. | Long-lived feature branches produce painful merges and hide integration risk until the end. Trunk-based development with feature flags is the modern default. A client's branching strategy is a readable signal of delivery health. | "Just roll it back" is trivial for code and hard-to-impossible for a database migration that already ran. Knowing that distinction is what keeps a Tier 3 from promising a rollback that isn't available. |
| **CI/CD** | On every change: build, run tests, run security scans, deploy. Mature teams deploy many times a day; less mature ones batch releases into scheduled windows. | **This is the fastest read available on a client's real velocity ceiling, and it's observable in week one.** A team releasing monthly cannot deliver a two-week feedback loop no matter what the roadmap says. Ask: how often do you deploy, how long does the pipeline take, and can any engineer trigger it? | Knight Capital lost roughly $440M in 45 minutes in August 2012 because a manual deployment reached only seven of eight servers. CrowdStrike's July 2024 incident took down millions of Windows machines worldwide through a content update with insufficient staged rollout. Release process *is* enterprise risk, at a magnitude executives recognize. |
| **Feature flags** | Runtime switches evaluated per user or segment. Enable gradual rollout, instant kill-switch, and A/B testing. | Decoupling deploy from release changes what a launch plan can look like — you can ship code weeks early and turn it on for 1% of users. It also creates flag debt: old flags that nobody removes become permanent hidden branches. | A kill-switch is a risk-mitigation control an executive audience understands immediately. It's often the difference between a launch that gets approved and one that doesn't. |
| **Observability** | Logs (what happened), metrics (how much), traces (the path of one request through many services). Plus alerting on top. | Without tracing, debugging a microservices problem is guesswork. If a client has microservices and no distributed tracing, that's a finding worth raising early — it predicts long incidents. | Mean time to detection and mean time to resolution are board-legible numbers. They're also the honest measure of whether a modernization actually improved anything. |
| **Automated testing** | Unit tests (one function), integration tests (components together), end-to-end tests (the whole user flow). | Test coverage is the ceiling on how confidently a team can change anything. A client asking for rapid iteration on an untested codebase is asking for two incompatible things — say so early, kindly, with a number. | Low coverage is a direct handoff risk. Per `_TORQ-COMPANY-CONTEXT.md`, the engagement's value has to survive after Torq leaves. Code the client's team is afraid to change doesn't survive. |
| **Code review / pull request** | Changes are proposed on a branch and reviewed before merging. Standard practice, and an audit trail in regulated environments. | Review turnaround time is a hidden velocity tax. If PRs sit for three days, that's a process problem with a bigger impact than most tooling changes. | In regulated industries, code review is frequently a *control* — segregation of duties, change-management evidence. Speeding it up has an approval dimension, not just a workflow one. |

## 10. Deep dive — Infrastructure

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** cloud means renting servers instead of owning them. Containers package an app so it runs the same everywhere. Kubernetes runs a lot of containers. All three are about how software gets to users, not what it does.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cloud (IaaS / PaaS / SaaS)** | IaaS rents raw machines, PaaS rents a managed platform, SaaS rents finished software. Most clients use all three simultaneously. | Managed services trade control for operational burden — a managed database costs more per month and saves an engineer's week. Usually worth it, and worth saying out loud when a client's team wants to self-host. | Cloud spend is now a board-level line item, and FinOps is a real practice. Cloud is also where lock-in is most consequential: proprietary managed services are the stickiest part of any provider relationship. Multi-cloud sounds like insurance and usually buys complexity instead. |
| **Infrastructure as code** | Declarative definitions (Terraform, CloudFormation) versioned alongside application code. | It's what makes environments reproducible and makes disaster recovery a rehearsed procedure rather than an improvisation. Its absence means environments have drifted and nobody knows how much. | Manually configured production infrastructure with no code definition is a continuity risk. If the person who built it leaves, the knowledge leaves. That's a named finding in any transformation assessment. |
| **Containers / Docker** | Package an application with its dependencies into an image that runs identically anywhere. Lighter than a virtual machine. | Solves environment drift. Doesn't solve architecture — containerizing a monolith gives you a portable monolith, which is sometimes exactly the right pragmatic step and should be described as such. | "Lift and shift into containers" is a legitimate, lower-risk first phase of modernization. It's also frequently sold as more transformative than it is. Set the expectation correctly at scoping time. |
| **Kubernetes** | Orchestrates container scheduling, scaling, networking, and self-healing across a cluster. | Powerful and genuinely complex. A small team running Kubernetes for three services has bought an operational burden they didn't need — a managed platform service would serve them better. | Kubernetes requires a platform capability the client must staff or buy. If a client wants it without that capability, the honest recommendation is a managed platform. That's a credibility-building pushback, and it's often the right one. |
| **CDN** | Geographically distributed edge caches serving static content and absorbing traffic near the user. | The cheapest global performance improvement available, and it doubles as DDoS absorption. Almost always a yes for a consumer-facing product. | Also a single point of failure. The June 2021 Fastly outage briefly took down large parts of the web — Reddit, Amazon, and major news sites — from one provider's configuration change. Concentration risk is real at the edge too. |
| **Open source** | Public code under a license. Licenses differ materially: permissive (MIT, Apache 2.0) versus copyleft (GPL) versus source-available (BSL). | "We'll use an open-source library" carries a maintenance obligation and a license question. Neither is usually in the estimate. | Log4Shell (December 2021) forced emergency patching across the entire enterprise Java ecosystem from a single library nobody had on a risk register. Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) requirements now follow from exactly that event. License compliance is separately a legal exposure — copyleft in a distributed product is a real problem, not a formality. |

## 11. Deep dive — AI and agentic tooling

**Tier 1, the one thing to remember:** an LLM predicts text. RAG is how you make it answer using *your* documents. An agent is a model that can take actions, not just answer. MCP is the standard plug for connecting models to tools. Evals are how you test whether any of it works.

This category ages fastest. Treat every row as current as of August 2026 and verify before quoting a client anything time-sensitive.

| Term | A level deeper | Tier 2 — the trade-off you can discuss | Tier 3 — strategic & risk read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LLM** | A transformer model trained to predict the next token. Capability comes from scale, training data, and post-training. It does not "look things up" unless you give it a tool that does. | The core distinction that resolves most client confusion: the model has *general* knowledge from training and *specific* knowledge only from what you put in its context. "It doesn't know our products" is a context problem, not a model problem. | Model choice is a build/buy decision with switching costs. Prompts, evals, and integrations are partly portable; a fine-tuned model is not. Architect for provider substitution unless there's a strong reason not to. |
| **Tokens / context window** | Text splits into tokens (roughly ¾ of a word in English). The context window bounds how much the model can consider at once. Pricing is per million input and output tokens. | This is where AI cost estimates come from. A per-request token estimate multiplied by expected volume gives you a monthly number — and that number is the difference between a feature that's viable and one that isn't. Do it during scoping, not after the pilot. | AI unit economics are frequently absent from a client's business case. The consultant who supplies the cost-per-transaction number before the pilot is the one who gets asked back for phase two. |
| **RAG** | Retrieve relevant documents, then include them in the model's context so the answer is grounded in real source material, with citations. Term and technique from Lewis et al. (2020). | The most transferable ownership boundary in AI product work: **engineers own retrieval mechanics; PMs own what should be retrieved and why.** Which documents are authoritative, which are stale, and who decides — those are product questions, and a client's team often has no owner for them. | RAG puts client data into a model's context. That is a data-governance question first: what's in the corpus, who can see it, does retrieval respect existing permissions? In a regulated client, this needs legal review before a pilot, not after. Permission leakage through retrieval is a genuine and under-appreciated exposure. |
| **Embeddings / vector DB** | Text becomes a numeric vector capturing meaning; similar meanings sit close together. The vector database finds nearest matches. | Explains why semantic search finds "cancel my plan" when the document says "terminate subscription." Also explains why retrieval quality is a tuning problem with real effort behind it — chunking, ranking, and thresholds all matter. | Re-embedding a large corpus when you change models is a real cost and a real schedule item. Ask about corpus size before agreeing to a model migration timeline. |
| **Fine-tuning** | Additional training on your own examples to shift behavior, format, or tone. | The most common client misunderstanding in AI scoping: fine-tuning is usually the wrong answer to "it doesn't know our facts." That's RAG. Fine-tuning is for consistent behavior and format. Correcting this early saves a client real money. | Fine-tuning creates the strongest form of provider lock-in available, plus an ongoing retraining obligation as the base model advances. It's a build/buy/tune decision, and it belongs in the SOW conversation, not in an engineering ticket. |
| **Agent** | The model chooses tools and sequences actions toward a goal, observing results and re-planning between steps. Distinguished from a fixed workflow by the fact that the model decides the path. | The honest boundary: agents excel where steps are variable and verification is cheap. They're a poor fit where a deterministic workflow would do the same job more reliably and more cheaply. "Could this be a script?" is a fair and useful question. | Agentic systems need a specification artifact — permitted tools, authority limits, escalation paths, audit trail. The AI PM line's Agent Workflow Spec is the pattern. An agent with unbounded permissions in a regulated environment is an unowned risk, and naming it is exactly the Tier 3 contribution. |
| **MCP** | Model Context Protocol — an open standard (JSON-RPC based) for exposing tools, data, and prompts to AI models. One server per system, usable by any compliant client. | It converts an N×M integration problem into N+M. Practically: a client's "we need custom AI integrations for each tool" plan may be outdated. Introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 and adopted by OpenAI in March 2025 — the cross-vendor adoption is what makes it a standard rather than a vendor feature. | An MCP server is an access-control boundary. Whatever it exposes, the model can use — so the security review is on the server's scope, not on the model. Get that framing right with a client's security team; it's usually the clarification that unblocks a pilot. |
| **Evals** | A test set of representative inputs with expected outputs or graded criteria, run on every prompt or model change to produce a measured pass rate. | Without evals, "we improved the prompt" is an unfalsifiable claim, and it will not survive a client readout. Building the eval set is a product job — it's a definition of what "correct" means for this feature. | **The accuracy trap is the single most useful number in a client AI conversation:** 95% accuracy on 1,000 transactions a day is 50 wrong outputs daily, some of them silently wrong. Ask what happens to those fifty. It converts an enthusiasm conversation into a risk conversation with arithmetic, which is what an executive audience actually responds to. |
| **Hallucination** | Confident, fluent, wrong output. A property of how these models work, mitigated by grounding and design, not eliminated. | Mitigations are largely UX decisions and therefore PM-owned: cite sources, show confidence, provide an escape hatch to a human, and design the failure case deliberately. | Liability. In healthcare, financial services, or aerospace, a confidently wrong output has consequences beyond a bad user experience. Human-in-the-loop is a design requirement in those settings, not a maturity stage to grow out of. |
| **AI coding assistant** | Tools ranging from autocomplete to agents that read a repository, write code, run tests, and open pull requests. | The effort profile shifts: less time writing, much more time specifying and verifying. That raises the value of a precise, unambiguous specification — which is a PM deliverable, and it's why spec-readiness matters more now, not less. | Anthropic's account of its Bun-runtime rewrite put roughly 15% of effort on writing code and 85% on verification. If that ratio generalizes, PM and QA leverage rises while raw implementation cost falls — which changes team shape, estimates, and what a client should be buying. Note the honest gap flagged in the Learning Path: **no course line currently teaches how product artifacts change when engineering is agentic.** This row is the closest the curriculum gets. |

## 12. Client-room phrasebook

What you'll actually hear, what it usually means, and the question that makes you useful rather than decorative.

| What they say | What it usually means | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "It's just a CSS change." | A visual tweak — or an unbounded audit of every page if there's no design system. | "Is there a design system, or is this styled per-page?" |
| "We'll just call their API." | Nobody has read the API documentation yet. | "What does it expose, what's the rate limit, what's the auth, and who owns the credentials?" |
| "That runs in the overnight job." | A cron job. The data is at best a day old. | "What time does it run, what happens when it fails, and does anyone get alerted?" |
| "It's just a CRUD app." | Genuinely simple — until workflow, approvals, and audit appear. | "Walk me through delete. Is it a real delete, or a status change for retention?" |
| "We're moving to microservices." | Could be a considered plan or a rewrite in disguise. | "What problem is that solving, and do we have the CI/CD and observability to operate it?" |
| "We need it to be real-time." | Anything from sub-second to "fresher than yesterday." | "What decision does someone make with this, and how stale can the data be before that decision goes wrong?" |
| "The AI will handle it." | An unvalidated capability assumption. | "What's the accuracy target, and what happens to the cases it gets wrong?" |
| "We can't test that in staging." | Staging doesn't mirror production — data, scale, or integrations. | "What's different, and what would it take to close the gap?" |
| "That's technical debt." | A real constraint, a negotiating position, or a shrug. | "What does it cost us per sprint, and what would it cost to fix?" |
| "It works on my machine." | An environment or dependency difference. | "What's different between your environment and staging?" |
| "We'll need a spike for that." | Engineering doesn't yet know how hard it is. | "What question will the spike answer, and how long do you need?" |
| "It's in the backlog." | It exists as a ticket. Nothing more is implied. | "Is it prioritized, or just recorded?" |

## 13. The four-step system read

Adapted from the worked system-design method in *Tech Simplified*. Reviewers consistently identify this repeatable structure — not any individual product's architecture — as the book's most reusable asset. Run it on a client's system in the first two weeks of an engagement. It is four questions, and you don't need to understand the answers deeply to learn a great deal from *how* they're answered.

| Step | Ask | What the answer tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Requirements** | What does the system do (functional), and how well must it do it (non-functional)? | Whether NFRs exist at all. If nobody can state a target for load, latency, or availability, the estimate has no floor and you've found your first real issue. |
| **2. High-level design** | What are the major pieces, and how do they talk to each other? | Whether anyone can draw it. If no current architecture diagram exists, that's a finding — and producing one is a genuinely valuable, highly visible early deliverable. |
| **3. Data and interfaces** | What's stored where, and what are the APIs between the pieces? | Where the integration risk and the compliance exposure actually live. |
| **4. Design choices against NFRs** | Which decisions were made to satisfy which requirement, and what did each cost? | Whether the architecture is deliberate or accumulated. Both are workable; they need completely different engagement plans. |

**Tier note.** Tier 1 observes and takes the notes. Tier 2 runs the conversation and produces the diagram. Tier 3 reads the answers as delivery risk and prices phase two accordingly.

## 14. What this guide is not, and where it came from

### Not

- **Not a coding course.** Nothing here teaches you to write anything. The target ceiling is L3 — understanding what a technical choice implies for the product.
- **Not an architecture opinion.** Recognition earns you the right to ask a good question, not to critique a client's stack.
- **Not current forever.** §11 (AI/agentic) ages in months. §3–§10 age in years. Re-verify §11 before quoting anything to a client.
- **Not a substitute for asking.** The best outcome of this guide is that you ask a better question, sooner, of the person who actually knows.
